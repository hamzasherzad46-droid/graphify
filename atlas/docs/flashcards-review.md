## 1. Sphenoid bone

*Core · Identify · Skull · id `sk-sphenoid-identify` · structure `sphenoid-bone` · 3D on front: yes*

**Objective.** Identify the sphenoid bone on the skull.

**Front.** Name the highlighted bone.

**Answer.** **Sphenoid bone**

**Why.** It is the single, complex bone in the centre of the skull base, and it reaches out to the sides of the skull. It is called the keystone bone because it joins with almost every other bone of the skull.

**Explain simpler.** It sits in the middle of the floor of the skull and touches almost every other skull bone, like the keystone of an arch.

**Go deeper.**

- Lesser wings: the ridge between the anterior and middle cranial fossae.
- Sella turcica: in the midline; its hypophyseal fossa holds the pituitary gland.
- Greater wings: the anterior floor of the middle cranial fossa and part of the side of the skull.
- Its body contains the sphenoid sinus, the most posterior paranasal sinus.
- Its greater wing is one of the four bones that meet at the pterion.

**Clinical.** Because the sphenoid sinus lies just in front of and below the sella turcica, surgeons can reach a pituitary tumour through the nose and the sphenoid sinus (transsphenoidal surgery), without opening the skull. †

**Memory hook.** “Sphenoid” comes from the Greek for wedge: it is wedged between the other bones of the skull. †

**Exam trap.** Do not confuse it with the ethmoid, the smaller midline bone in front of it whose cribriform plates form the roof of the nasal cavity.

**Source.** OpenStax A&P 2e, 7.2 The Skull. Paragraphs used: s7-2#fs-id1248497, s7-2#fs-id1469126, s7-2#fs-id1882870, s7-2#fs-id1977415.

**Needs confirming.**

- “Because the sphenoid sinus lies just in front of and below the sella turcica, surgeons can…”: Transsphenoidal surgery is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20); the sinus position is.
- ““Sphenoid” comes from the Greek for wedge: it is wedged between the other bones of the sku…”: Etymology (Greek sphēn, wedge) is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).

## 2. Occipital bone

*Core · Feature · Skull · id `sk-foramen-magnum` · structure `foramen-magnum` · 3D on front: no*

**Objective.** State which bone contains the foramen magnum.

**Front.** Which bone contains the foramen magnum?

**Answer.** **Occipital bone**

**Why.** The occipital bone forms the back of the skull and the back of its base. The foramen magnum opens in that base so that the spinal cord can leave the skull.

**Explain simpler.** The bone at the back of the head has a big hole underneath, and the spinal cord passes through it.

**Go deeper.**

- Lies in the midline of the posterior cranial fossa, the deepest of the three fossae.
- The posterior cranial fossa holds the cerebellum.
- Occipital condyles on either side form joints with the first cervical vertebra.
- The hypoglossal canals open at its anterolateral margin, carrying the nerve to the tongue.

**Clinical.** Raised pressure inside the skull can push the lowest part of the cerebellum (the tonsils) down through the foramen magnum, compressing the brainstem: “coning”, which can stop breathing. †

**Exam trap.** The foramen magnum is in the occipital bone, not the temporal bone: the temporal bone’s opening beside the posterior fossa is the internal acoustic meatus.

**Source.** OpenStax A&P 2e, 7.2 The Skull. Paragraphs used: s7-2#fs-id1478275, s7-2#fs-id1489044, s7-2#fs-id1724550.

**Needs confirming.**

- “Raised pressure inside the skull can push the lowest part of the cerebellum (the tonsils) …”: Tonsillar herniation is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).

## 3. Middle meningeal artery (anterior branch)

*Distinction · Supply · Skull · id `sk-pterion-artery` · structure `pterion` · 3D on front: no*

**Objective.** Name the artery that lies deep to the pterion.

**Front.** Which artery lies immediately deep to the pterion?

**Answer.** **Middle meningeal artery (anterior branch)**

**Why.** The pterion is where four skull bones meet, and it is the weakest part of the skull. A major branch of the artery that supplies the skull and the coverings of the brain runs on its inner surface, so a blow here can fracture the bone and tear the artery. †

**Explain simpler.** The pterion is a thin spot on the side of the head. An important artery runs just inside it, so a hard knock there can break the bone and the artery.

**Go deeper.**

- Four bones meet in an H-shaped suture: frontal, parietal, temporal (squamous part) and sphenoid (greater wing).
- Surface marking: about two finger-widths above the zygomatic arch, a thumb’s width behind the zygomatic bone.
- The artery enters the skull through the foramen spinosum. †
- Its branches groove the inner surface of the skull.
- It is a branch of the maxillary artery. †

**Clinical.** A torn artery bleeds between the skull and the dura: an extradural haematoma. The patient may seem well at first, but the growing clot presses on the brain and can kill within hours, so it is a surgical emergency. †

**Exam trap.** An extradural haematoma is usually arterial (middle meningeal artery); a subdural haematoma is usually venous (bridging veins). †

**Source.** OpenStax A&P 2e, 7.2 The Skull. Paragraphs used: s7-2#fs-id1248497, s7-2#fs-id1364278, s7-2#fs-id2026215.

**Needs confirming.**

- “The pterion is where four skull bones meet, and it is the weakest part of the skull. A maj…”: OpenStax does not name the artery; “middle meningeal” needs an approved source.
- “The artery enters the skull through the foramen spinosum.”: OpenStax describes the artery of the foramen spinosum without naming it.
- “It is a branch of the maxillary artery.”: Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).
- “A torn artery bleeds between the skull and the dura: an extradural haematoma. The patient …”: OpenStax says “hematoma between the brain and interior of the skull”; the term “extradural” needs an approved source.

## 4. Cribriform plate

*Core · Reverse · Skull · id `sk-cribriform-reverse` · structure `cribriform-plate` · 3D on front: no*

**Objective.** Recognise the cribriform plate from its description.

**Front.** Name it: a small, flat, sieve-like part of the ethmoid bone in the roof of the nasal cavity, pierced by openings for olfactory nerve branches.

**Answer.** **Cribriform plate**

**Why.** “Cribrum” means sieve: the plate is pierced by many small olfactory foramina. Nerve branches from the smell-sensing area of the nasal cavity pass up through them to enter the brain.

**Explain simpler.** It is a thin plate full of tiny holes, like a sieve. The nerves for smell pass through the holes from the nose to the brain.

**Go deeper.**

- Lies on either side of the crista galli, in the midline of the anterior cranial fossa.
- Forms the narrow roof of the nasal cavity.
- The crista galli gives anterior attachment to one of the coverings of the brain.
- The anterior cranial fossa, which it helps to floor, holds the frontal lobes.

**Clinical.** A fracture through the cribriform plate can tear the olfactory nerve fibres, causing loss of smell, and can let cerebrospinal fluid leak from the nose. †

**Exam trap.** Do not mix up the cribriform plate (ethmoid bone, smell) with the optic canal (sphenoid bone, sight).

**Source.** OpenStax A&P 2e, 7.2 The Skull. Paragraphs used: s13-4#fs-id1521213, s14-1#fs-id2817840, s7-2#fs-id1977415, s7-2#fs-id2026215, s7-2#fs-id2045358.

**Needs confirming.**

- “A fracture through the cribriform plate can tear the olfactory nerve fibres, causing loss …”: OpenStax confirms that head trauma can shear the olfactory axons and cause loss of smell (anosmia); a fracture of the cribriform plate and a leak of cerebrospinal fluid from the nose are not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).

## 5. Scaphoid

*Higher · Clinical · Upper limb · id `ul-scaphoid-foosh` · structure `scaphoid` · 3D on front: no*

**Objective.** Recognise a scaphoid fracture from its mechanism and site of tenderness.

**Front.** A student falls on an outstretched hand and has tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox. Which bone is most likely fractured?

**Answer.** **Scaphoid**

**Why.** The scaphoid links the proximal and distal rows of carpal bones and meets the radius directly, so it takes much of the force of a fall on the outstretched hand. It lies in the floor of the anatomical snuffbox, so tenderness there points to it. †

**Explain simpler.** The scaphoid is the wrist bone on the thumb side that meets the radius, so a fall onto the hand loads it heavily. It sits under the small hollow at the base of the thumb, so pain there suggests it is broken. †

**Go deeper.**

- The most commonly fractured carpal bone.
- Its blood supply (from branches of the radial artery) enters mainly at the distal end. †
- So a fracture across the waist can cut off the supply to the proximal pole. †
- Risk: avascular necrosis and non-union of the proximal fragment. †
- The first X-ray can look normal; a later X-ray, once the swelling settles, shows the fracture.

**Clinical.** A missed scaphoid fracture can lead to avascular necrosis and later degenerative arthritis of the wrist, because its blood supply is poor. That is why a suspected fracture is treated with caution even when the first X-ray is clear.

**Exam trap.** Do not confuse it with a Colles’ fracture of the distal radius: also from a fall on the outstretched hand, but with a “dinner fork” deformity above the wrist rather than snuffbox tenderness.

**Source.** OpenStax A&P 2e, 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb. Paragraphs used: s8-2#fs-id1882937, s8-2#fs-id1977414, s8-2#fs-id1989621, s8-2#fs-id2142217, s8-2#fs-id2637051.

**Needs confirming.**

- “The scaphoid links the proximal and distal rows of carpal bones and meets the radius direc…”: “Links the two rows” and “floor of the anatomical snuffbox” are not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).
- “The scaphoid is the wrist bone on the thumb side that meets the radius, so a fall onto the…”: The hollow at the base of the thumb (snuffbox) is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).
- “Its blood supply (from branches of the radial artery) enters mainly at the distal end.”: Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).
- “So a fracture across the waist can cut off the supply to the proximal pole.”: Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).
- “Risk: avascular necrosis and non-union of the proximal fragment.”: OpenStax says “bone necrosis”; “non-union” and “proximal fragment” need an approved source.

## 6. Ulnar nerve

*Higher · Relation · Upper limb · id `ul-ulnar-nerve-epicondyle` · structure `ulnar-nerve` · 3D on front: yes*

**Objective.** Name the nerve that lies directly posterior to the medial epicondyle.

**Front.** Which nerve lies directly posterior to the medial epicondyle of the humerus?

**Answer.** **Ulnar nerve**

**Why.** The ulnar nerve runs in a shallow groove on the back of the medial epicondyle, covered only by skin and fascia. That is why a knock there sends tingling down the little-finger side of the hand. †

**Explain simpler.** The nerve passes right behind the bony bump on the inner side of the elbow, just under the skin, so hitting it makes the little finger tingle. †

**Go deeper.**

- The medial epicondyle is the origin of the powerful anterior forearm (grasping) muscles.
- The nerve enters the forearm between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris. †
- It supplies most of the small (intrinsic) muscles of the hand. †
- It gives sensation to the little finger and the medial half of the ring finger. †

**Clinical.** A fracture of the medial epicondyle, or long pressure on the elbow, can injure the ulnar nerve, weakening the small muscles of the hand and numbing the little finger. †

**Memory hook.** The “funny bone” is not a bone: it is the ulnar nerve behind the medial epicondyle. †

**Exam trap.** Only the ulnar nerve lies behind the medial epicondyle: the median nerve crosses in front of the elbow, and the radial nerve lies on the lateral side. †

**Source.** OpenStax A&P 2e, 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb (bone); nerve relations await an approved source. Paragraphs used: s8-2#fs-id1949647.

## 7. A strut holding the shoulder out from the trunk

*Higher · Function · Upper limb · id `ul-clavicle-strut` · structure `clavicle` · 3D on front: no*

**Objective.** Explain the main mechanical role of the clavicle.

**Front.** What is the main mechanical role of the clavicle?

**Answer.** **A strut holding the shoulder out from the trunk**

**Why.** Anchored by muscles from above, the clavicle reaches out to support the scapula, holding the shoulder joint up and away from the trunk. This gives the upper limb its wide range of movement, and passes forces from the limb to the sternum and axial skeleton.

**Explain simpler.** The collarbone works like a prop: it holds the shoulder out to the side so the arm can move freely, and passes knocks on the arm to the breastbone.

**Go deeper.**

- The only long bone that lies horizontally.
- Sternoclavicular joint: the only bony joint between the upper limb and the axial skeleton.
- Its lateral (acromial) end articulates with the acromion of the scapula.
- The costoclavicular ligament to the first rib supports the sternoclavicular joint.
- It protects the nerves and vessels passing between the trunk and the upper limb.

**Clinical.** The clavicle is the most commonly fractured bone, usually between its middle and lateral parts. With the strut broken, the shoulder drops under the weight of the limb, so the patient supports the arm with the other hand.

**Exam trap.** The upper limb’s only bony link to the axial skeleton is the sternoclavicular joint, not the shoulder (glenohumeral) joint.

**Source.** OpenStax A&P 2e, 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle. Paragraphs used: s8-1#fs-id1192235, s8-1#fs-id1866400, s8-1#fs-id2241809.

## 8. Radius

*Core · Compare · Upper limb · id `ul-radius-lateral` · structure `radius` · 3D on front: no*

**Objective.** State which forearm bone is lateral in the anatomical position.

**Front.** Radius or ulna: which is lateral in the anatomical position?

**Answer.** **Radius**

**Why.** In the anatomical position the palm faces forward, so the thumb points laterally. The radius runs on the thumb side of the forearm, parallel to the ulna on the little-finger side.

**Explain simpler.** Stand with your palms facing forward: the radius is the forearm bone on the thumb side, on the outside.

**Go deeper.**

- The radial head articulates with the capitulum of the humerus.
- The ulna’s trochlear notch articulates with the trochlea, the main hinge of the elbow.
- Distally the radius forms the wrist joint with the scaphoid and lunate; the ulna does not touch the carpal bones.
- The radial styloid reaches further distally than the ulnar styloid.

**Clinical.** A Colles’ fracture of the distal radius, the most common forearm fracture, follows a fall on the outstretched hand. The distal fragment is driven backwards and upwards, giving the wrist its “dinner fork” bend.

**Exam trap.** In pronation the radius crosses over the ulna, but sides are always named in the anatomical position, where the radius is lateral.

**Source.** OpenStax A&P 2e, 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb; 9.5 Types of Body Movements. Paragraphs used: s8-2#fs-id1411842, s8-2#fs-id1882937, s8-2#fs-id2080518, s8-2#fs-id2142217, s9-5#fs-id1547390, s9-5#fs-id1837207.

## 9. Axillary nerve

*Distinction · Relation · Upper limb · id `ul-axillary-surgical-neck` · structure `axillary-nerve` · 3D on front: yes*

**Objective.** Name the nerve at risk in a fracture of the surgical neck of the humerus.

**Front.** Which nerve is most at risk in a fracture of the surgical neck of the humerus?

**Answer.** **Axillary nerve**

**Why.** The surgical neck is where the expanded upper end of the humerus narrows into the shaft, and it is a common site of fracture. The axillary nerve wraps around the back of this neck, so a fracture here can stretch or tear it. †

**Explain simpler.** The surgical neck is the narrow part just below the ball of the humerus. A nerve wraps around it, so breaking the bone there can damage the nerve. †

**Go deeper.**

- A fall can drive the shaft into the head: an impacted fracture.
- The posterior circumflex humeral artery runs around the neck with the nerve. †
- The axillary nerve supplies the deltoid and teres minor. †
- It supplies the skin over the lower deltoid (the “regimental badge” area). †
- Lower down, the radial nerve lies in the radial groove, at risk in shaft fractures. †

**Clinical.** Damage to the axillary nerve weakens the deltoid, so raising the arm out to the side is weak, with numbness over the lower outer shoulder. †

**Exam trap.** Surgical neck (below the tubercles, often fractured, axillary nerve) is not the anatomical neck (the margin of the smooth head).

**Source.** OpenStax A&P 2e, 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb (bone); nerve relations await an approved source. Paragraphs used: s20-5#fs-id1595346, s8-2#fs-id2030259, s8-2#fs-id2110691.

## 10. The flexor retinaculum

*Higher · Feature · Upper limb · id `ul-carpal-tunnel-roof` · structure `flexor-retinaculum` · 3D on front: no*

**Objective.** State what forms the roof of the carpal tunnel.

**Front.** What forms the roof of the carpal tunnel?

**Answer.** **The flexor retinaculum**

**Why.** The carpal bones form a U-shaped grouping that makes the walls and floor of the tunnel. The flexor retinaculum, a strong ligament, spans the top of the U as the roof and holds the bones in their arch.

**Explain simpler.** The wrist bones make a U shape. A strong band across the top of the U closes it into a tunnel.

**Go deeper.**

- Attached laterally to the scaphoid and trapezium, and medially to the hamate and pisiform.
- Carries the tendons of nine anterior forearm muscles and the median nerve into the hand. †
- Overuse or injury can make the contents swell inside this fixed space.

**Clinical.** Swelling in the tunnel compresses the median nerve: carpal tunnel syndrome, with pain or numbness and weakness in the parts of the hand it supplies. The bony walls and tough roof cannot stretch, so any swelling squeezes the nerve. †

**Exam trap.** The flexor retinaculum is the roof, not the floor: the carpal bones form the floor and walls.

**Source.** OpenStax A&P 2e, 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb. Paragraphs used: s8-2#fs-id2329218.

**Needs confirming.**

- “Carries the tendons of nine anterior forearm muscles and the median nerve into the hand.”: OpenStax says “an important nerve”; naming it the median nerve needs an approved source.
- “Swelling in the tunnel compresses the median nerve: carpal tunnel syndrome, with pain or n…”: OpenStax does not name the nerve; “median” needs an approved source.
