### 1.1 Humerus

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb; 8.5; 9.6; 20.5. Status: draft.*

**1. In one line**

- The long bone of the upper arm, running from the shoulder to the elbow (humerus). <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691; OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1415448]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* The humerus is the bone of your upper arm. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* It is a long bone: a hollow shaft (diaphysis) with a wider end at each end (epiphysis). <small>[OpenStax 6.3 Bone Structure, ¶fs-id1025647]</small>

**2. Where it is**

- It sits in the arm, between the shoulder above and the elbow below. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1415448]</small>
- Its round head faces inwards (medially) and fits the shallow socket of the shoulder blade. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691]</small>
- At the elbow it meets the ulna on the inner side and the radius on the outer side. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2444175]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* It runs from your shoulder down to your elbow. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1415448]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* It forms the shoulder (glenohumeral) joint above and part of the elbow joint below. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2305343; OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2444175]</small>

**3. Key features**

- **Head.** The large, smooth, round top. It forms the shoulder joint with the glenoid cavity of the scapula. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691]</small>
- **Anatomical neck.** The edge of the smooth head. It marks where the joint surface ends. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691]</small>
- **Greater and lesser tubercles.** Two bumps near the top. The rotator cuff muscles attach to them. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691; OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2429784]</small>
- **Intertubercular (bicipital) groove.** The groove between the tubercles. A tendon of biceps brachii runs in it. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691]</small>
- **Surgical neck.** Where the wide top narrows into the shaft. It is a common site of fracture. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691]</small>
- **Deltoid tuberosity.** A rough V-shaped area halfway down the outer shaft. The deltoid muscle attaches here. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691]</small>
- **Medial and lateral epicondyles.** Bony points either side of the elbow. Forearm muscles start from them. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1949647]</small>
- **Trochlea and capitulum.** The pulley-shaped trochlea meets the ulna. The knob-like capitulum meets the radius. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2444175]</small>
- **Olecranon fossa.** A hollow at the back. It takes the tip of the ulna when the elbow straightens. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2444175]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* Its top end makes the shoulder joint and its bottom end makes the elbow joint. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691; OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2444175]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* The bone usually breaks at the surgical neck, not the anatomical neck. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691; OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2030259]</small>

*Go deeper:*

**4. Form → function**

- The big round head sits in a socket about a third of its size. This gives the shoulder its huge range of movement. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1231336; OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2305343]</small>
- The price is stability: the shoulder relies on muscles, not bone, to hold the head in place. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2305343; OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2429784]</small>
- The medial epicondyle is larger than the lateral one. The strong gripping muscles of the forearm pull on it. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1949647]</small>
- Hollows above the elbow surfaces make room for the forearm bones. So the elbow can bend and straighten fully. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2444175]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* Its shape lets the shoulder move freely and the elbow bend and straighten fully. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2305343; OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2444175]</small>

**5. Relations**

- *muscle*, **Rotator cuff.** Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis attach to the tubercles. They hold the head in its socket. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2429784]</small>
- *muscle*, **Deltoid.** Attaches to the deltoid tuberosity on the outer shaft. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691]</small>
- *muscle*, **Biceps brachii.** One of its tendons runs in the intertubercular groove. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691]</small>
- *artery*, **Humeral circumflex arteries.** Branches of the axillary artery that supply the area near the head. <small>[OpenStax 20.5 Circulatory Pathways, ¶fs-id1595346]</small>
- *artery*, **Brachial artery.** Runs down the arm and splits near the elbow into the radial and ulnar arteries. <small>[OpenStax 20.5 Circulatory Pathways, ¶fs-id1595346]</small>
- *nerves*, **Four major nerves.** Four major nerves of the upper limb lie close to the humerus. Fractures can damage them. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2166864]</small>
- *nerve*, **Axillary nerve.** <mark>† Winds around the surgical neck.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>
- *nerve*, **Radial nerve.** <mark>† Lies in the radial (spiral) groove on the back of the shaft.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>
- *nerve*, **Ulnar nerve.** <mark>† Passes behind the medial epicondyle.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>
- *Explain simpler:* Muscles that move the shoulder attach near the top. Important nerves and arteries run close to the bone. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2429784; OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2166864]</small>

**6. Development**

- It forms by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification), like all limb bones except the clavicle. <small>[OpenStax 8.5 Development of the Appendicular Skeleton, ¶fs-id1397163]</small>
- Bone first appears in the shaft by about the twelfth week. Centres in the ends appear later, usually after birth. <small>[OpenStax 8.5 Development of the Appendicular Skeleton, ¶fs-id1397163]</small>
- Growth plates between the shaft and ends let the bone lengthen. They close at adult size. <small>[OpenStax 8.5 Development of the Appendicular Skeleton, ¶fs-id1397163]</small>

**7. Variations**

- <mark>† A few people have a small hook of bone above the medial epicondyle (supracondylar process). It can press on the median nerve.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>

**8. Clinical reasoning**

1. **Injury:** A fall onto the hand or elbow breaks the bone across the surgical neck. **Damaged:** The surgical neck; the shaft can be driven into the head (impacted fracture). **Patient can’t:** Move the shoulder without pain. **Why:** The narrow neck is where the wide top meets the shaft, so force concentrates there. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2030259; OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691]</small>
2. <mark>† Injury: A fracture of the middle of the shaft. Damaged: The radial nerve in the radial groove. Patient can’t: Lift the wrist and fingers (wrist drop). Why: The radial nerve supplies the muscles on the back of the forearm that extend the wrist and fingers.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>
3. <mark>† Injury: A child falls on an outstretched hand with the elbow straight (the commonest type). The bone breaks just above the epicondyles (supracondylar fracture). Damaged: The artery to the forearm (brachial artery), squeezed by swelling. Patient can’t: Keep the forearm muscles healthy: without blood they can quickly be damaged for good. Why: The brachial artery runs down to the elbow right beside the broken bone.</mark><br><small>**Partly sourced:** OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2166864; OpenStax 20.5 Circulatory Pathways, ¶fs-id1595346; Atlas academic reviewer, 25 November 2026. **Not found:** Mechanism: OpenStax 8.2 describes a fall onto the tip of the elbow. The reviewer gives the commonest type, a fall on an outstretched hand with the elbow straight; this needs an approved source.</small>
4. **Injury:** A fall onto the hand or elbow with the arm raised fully out to the side. **Damaged:** The shoulder joint: the head of the humerus usually dislocates forwards and downwards (anterior dislocation, over 95% of cases). **Patient can’t:** Move the shoulder. **Why:** The underside of the joint has the least support. The head is forced out below it and comes to rest in front. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1894060; Atlas academic reviewer, 25 November 2026]</small>

**9. Tutorial questions**

1. *Predict.* <mark>† Predict the deficit if the radial nerve is damaged in the radial (spiral) groove, and explain why.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>

   Model answer:

   - Wrist drop: the patient cannot lift (extend) the wrist.
   - The fingers cannot be straightened at the knuckles.
   - The radial nerve supplies the extensor muscles on the back of the forearm.
   - Triceps is usually spared: its branches leave the nerve above the groove.
   - Numbness over the back of the hand near the thumb.

2. *Explain.* Explain why the shoulder usually dislocates forwards and downwards (anterior dislocation). <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1231336; OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1894060; Atlas academic reviewer, 25 November 2026]</small>

   Model answer:

   - The glenoid cavity is small and shallow: about a third of the size of the head.
   - The underside of the joint has the least support.
   - Force on a fully raised arm drives the head out through the weak underside.
   - It comes to rest forwards and downwards: anterior dislocation, over 95% of cases.

3. *Compare.* Compare the anatomical neck and the surgical neck of the humerus. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2110691; OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id2030259]</small>

   Model answer:

   - Anatomical neck: the edge of the smooth joint surface of the head.
   - Surgical neck: where the wide top joins the narrow shaft.
   - The surgical neck is a common fracture site.
   - Fractures there can be impacted, with the shaft driven into the head.

### 1.2 Scapula

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle; 8.5; 9.6. Status: draft.*

**1. In one line**

- The flat, triangular shoulder blade at the back of the shoulder (scapula). <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1471081]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* The scapula is your shoulder blade. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1471081]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* With the clavicle it forms the shoulder (pectoral) girdle, which anchors the arm to the trunk. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1371880; OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1968095]</small>

**2. Where it is**

- It lies at the back of the shoulder. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1471081]</small>
- Muscles lie in front of it and behind it, so it does not touch the ribs. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1968095]</small>
- Outwards, it meets the humerus at the shoulder joint and the clavicle at the tip of the shoulder. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1471081; OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1475196]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* It sits on your upper back, behind your shoulder. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1471081]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* It has no joint with the ribs. Muscles cover it on both sides. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1968095]</small>

**3. Key features**

- **Glenoid cavity.** The shallow socket for the head of the humerus. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1725063]</small>
- **Spine.** The ridge across the back. With the acromion and clavicle it makes a V-shaped line for muscles. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1475196]</small>
- **Acromion.** The flat outer end of the spine. It is the bony tip of the shoulder and meets the clavicle. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1475196]</small>
- **Coracoid process.** A hook at the front shaped like a crow’s beak. It anchors chest and arm muscles and a strong ligament. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1475196]</small>
- **Supraspinous, infraspinous and subscapular fossae.** Broad hollows. They give large areas for muscles that move the arm. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1989073]</small>
- **Inferior angle.** The lowest corner. Several powerful muscles of the shoulder and arm attach here. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1725063]</small>
- **Supraglenoid and infraglenoid tubercles.** Small bumps above and below the socket, for arm muscles. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1725063]</small>
- **Suprascapular notch.** <mark>† A notch in the top border. The suprascapular nerve passes through it.</mark><br><small>**Partly sourced:** OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1725063. **Not found:** OpenStax gives the notch’s position; the nerve passing through it is not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>
- *Explain simpler:* It has a shallow socket for the arm bone, a ridge across the back and two hooks at the top. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1725063; OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1475196]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* The spine, acromion and clavicle form a V-shaped line where neck and back muscles attach. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1475196]</small>

*Go deeper:*

**4. Form → function**

- Its socket is shallow, so the arm can move very widely. The cost is a less stable joint. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2305343; OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1231336]</small>
- A small rim of fibrocartilage (glenoid labrum) deepens the socket slightly. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1231336]</small>
- Its broad hollows and ridges give big areas for the muscles that move and steady the arm. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1989073; OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1475196]</small>
- It is not joined to the ribs by bone. It moves with the clavicle as one unit. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1968095; OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1471081]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* Its shallow socket lets your arm move freely, and its wide surfaces hold strong muscles. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2305343; OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1989073]</small>

**5. Relations**

- *muscle*, **Rotator cuff.** Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis start on the scapula and attach to the humerus. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2429784]</small>
- *joint*, **Shoulder (glenohumeral) joint.** Ball-and-socket joint between the glenoid cavity and the head of the humerus. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2305343]</small>
- *joint*, **Acromioclavicular joint.** Where the acromion meets the clavicle. Its own ligaments are fairly weak. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1200622]</small>
- *ligament*, **Coracoclavicular ligament.** A very strong band from the coracoid to the clavicle. It gives the acromioclavicular joint its main support. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1200622]</small>
- *bursa*, **Subacromial bursa.** A fluid sac that stops the rotator cuff tendons rubbing on the scapula. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2429784]</small>
- *nerve*, **Long thoracic nerve.** <mark>† Supplies serratus anterior, which holds the scapula against the chest wall.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>
- *Explain simpler:* The muscles that hold the shoulder joint together start on the scapula. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2429784]</small>

**6. Development**

- It forms by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification), like all girdle bones except the clavicle. <small>[OpenStax 8.5 Development of the Appendicular Skeleton, ¶fs-id1397163]</small>

**7. Variations**

- <mark>† In some adults part of the acromion never fuses to the rest (os acromiale). It can be mistaken for a fracture.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>

**8. Clinical reasoning**

1. **Injury:** A strong blow to the outer shoulder, such as a player driven into the boards. **Damaged:** The acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular ligaments tear (a “shoulder separation”). **Patient can’t:** Hold the shoulder up: it droops. **Why:** The scapula separates from the clavicle and the weight of the arm pulls it down. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1200622]</small>
2. **Injury:** Repeated use with the arm raised out to the side, as in throwing, swimming or racquet sports. **Damaged:** The rotator cuff tendons, bursae or glenoid labrum. **Patient can’t:** Raise and use the arm without pain. **Why:** These tendons and the bursae become inflamed or torn with repeated use in raising the arm. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1894060]</small>
3. <mark>† Injury: Injury to the long thoracic nerve. Damaged: Serratus anterior. Patient can’t: Push against a wall without the scapula sticking out (“winging”). Why: Serratus anterior holds the scapula flat against the chest wall.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>

**9. Tutorial questions**

1. *Explain.* Explain how the shape of the scapula trades stability for mobility at the shoulder. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1231336; OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2429784]</small>

   Model answer:

   - The glenoid cavity is small and shallow: about a third of the size of the humeral head.
   - The joint capsule is thin and loose.
   - The glenoid labrum deepens the socket only slightly.
   - The rotator cuff muscles give the main support, acting as “dynamic ligaments”.

2. *Predict.* A rugby player lands on the point of the shoulder. Predict the injury and what you would see. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1200622]</small>

   Model answer:

   - Both the acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular ligaments rupture.
   - The acromion is forced under the end of the clavicle.
   - The shoulder droops as the weight of the arm pulls the scapula down.

3. *Compare.* Compare the coracoid process and the acromion. <small>[OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1475196; OpenStax 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle, ¶fs-id1200622]</small>

   Model answer:

   - Coracoid: a hook at the front that curves forwards and outwards.
   - It anchors chest and arm muscles and the coracoclavicular ligament.
   - Acromion: the flat outer end of the spine of the scapula.
   - It forms the tip of the shoulder and meets the clavicle.

### 1.3 Scaphoid

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb; 8.5. Status: draft.*

**1. In one line**

- A small boat-shaped wrist bone on the thumb side of the first row of carpal bones (scaphoid). <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1977414]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* The scaphoid is a small wrist bone at the base of the thumb. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1977414]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* The scaphoid and lunate meet the radius at the wrist joint; the triquetrum meets the articular disc over the ulna. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1882937; Atlas academic reviewer, 25 November 2026]</small>

**2. Where it is**

- It is the outermost bone of the first (proximal) row of carpal bones, on the thumb side. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1977414]</small>
- Above it is the radius. Beside it, on the inner side, is the lunate. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1882937; OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1977414]</small>
- Below it are the trapezium, trapezoid and capitate of the second row. <small>[OpenStax glossary: “scaphoid”]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* It sits in your wrist, just below the end of the forearm, on the thumb side. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1977414; OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1882937]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* With the lunate it meets the radius directly. The triquetrum, beside them, meets the articular disc over the ulna. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1882937; Atlas academic reviewer, 25 November 2026]</small>

**3. Key features**

- **Boat shape.** “Scaphoid” means boat-shaped. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1977414]</small>
- **Surface for the radius.** It meets the end of the radius directly at the wrist joint. <small>[OpenStax 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb, ¶fs-id1882937]</small>

### 1.4 Sphenoid bone

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 7.2 The Skull; 6.4; 14.2. Status: draft.*

**1. In one line**

- A single, complex bone in the centre of the skull base that joins almost every other skull bone (sphenoid bone). <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* The sphenoid is a winged bone in the middle of the floor of the skull. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* It is one of the eight bones of the brain case. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1351960]</small>

**2. Where it is**

- It forms much of the floor of the middle of the skull base. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>
- Its outer parts reach the sides of the skull, just in front of the temporal bone. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>
- Behind each eye, it forms the back wall of the eye socket (orbit). <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1887927]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* The sphenoid sits deep in the middle of your head, behind your eyes. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1887927]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* Because it joins with almost every other bone of the skull, it is called a “keystone” bone. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>

**3. Key features**

- **Lesser wings.** Form the ridge between the front and middle hollows (fossae) of the skull floor. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>
- **Greater wings.** Form the front of the middle fossa and part of the side of the skull. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>
- **Sella turcica.** A saddle-shaped seat in the midline. Its hollow (hypophyseal fossa) holds the pituitary gland. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>
- **Optic canal.** Beside the sella turcica. It carries the optic nerve from the eye to the brain. <small>[OpenStax glossary: “optic canal”; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215]</small>
- **Superior orbital fissure.** A slit between the lesser and greater wings of the sphenoid, into the back of the orbit. It carries nerves to the eye muscles and the forehead. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215; Atlas academic reviewer, 25 November 2026]</small>
- **Foramen rotundum, foramen ovale and foramen spinosum.** <mark>† Openings in the floor of the middle fossa for nerves to the face and an artery to the brain’s coverings.</mark><br><small>**Partly sourced:** OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215. **Not found:** OpenStax places these in the floor of the middle cranial fossa; that they lie in the sphenoid’s greater wing is not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>
- **Medial and lateral pterygoid plates.** Thin wing-shaped plates underneath. The lateral plates anchor chewing muscles. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1355530]</small>
- **Sphenoid sinus.** An air-filled space in its body: the most posterior paranasal sinus. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1882870]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* It has wings, a saddle that holds the pituitary gland and holes for nerves to the eye and face. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* Its lesser wings mark the border between the front (anterior) and middle cranial fossae. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>

*Go deeper:*

**4. Form → function**

- Its central position lets it act as a keystone, linking almost every other bone of the skull. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>
- Nerves and vessels pass through its openings between the brain and the eye, face and jaws. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215]</small>
- Its air-filled sinus lightens the skull and adds resonance to the voice. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1977054; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1882870]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* It locks the skull bones together and lets nerves pass through to the face. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215]</small>

**5. Relations**

- *gland*, **Pituitary gland.** Sits in the hypophyseal fossa of the sella turcica. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>
- *nerve*, **Optic nerve.** Passes through the optic canal. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215]</small>
- *nerves*, **Nerves to the eye muscles.** Pass through the superior orbital fissure. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215]</small>
- *nerve*, **Maxillary nerve.** <mark>† Leaves through the foramen rotundum to supply the cheek, nose and upper teeth.</mark><br><small>**Partly sourced:** OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215. **Not found:** OpenStax describes the nerve without naming it; “maxillary nerve” needs an approved source.</small>
- *artery*, **Middle meningeal artery.** <mark>† Enters through the foramen spinosum to supply the coverings of the brain.</mark><br><small>**Partly sourced:** OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215. **Not found:** OpenStax describes the artery without naming it; “middle meningeal artery” needs an approved source.</small>
- *muscles*, **Chewing muscles.** Attach to the lateral pterygoid plates. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1355530]</small>
- *bones*, **Pterion.** Its greater wing is one of four bones meeting at the pterion, the thinnest part of the skull. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1248497]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* The pituitary gland sits on it, and nerves to the eye and face pass through it. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215]</small>

**6. Development**

- Bones of the skull base form by replacing cartilage with bone (endochondral ossification). <small>[OpenStax 6.4 Bone Formation and Development, ¶fs-id1966864]</small>

**7. Variations**

- <mark>† The size of the sphenoid sinus varies a great deal from person to person.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>

**8. Clinical reasoning**

1. **Injury:** A tumour of the pituitary gland grows upwards out of the sella turcica. **Damaged:** The optic chiasm, where nerve fibres from the two eyes cross. **Patient can’t:** See objects to the right and left: the outer parts of both visual fields are lost. **Why:** The crossing fibres sit just above the pituitary gland and are pressed on first. <small>[OpenStax 14.2 Central Processing, ¶fs-id2365874; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>
2. **Injury:** A hard blow to the side of the head at the pterion. **Damaged:** The artery just inside the skull there; blood collects and presses on the brain. **Patient can’t:** Stay well: the patient may seem fine at first, then worsen and can die within hours without treatment. **Why:** The pterion is the thinnest part of the skull, with a major artery right under it. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1364278; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1248497]</small>

**9. Tutorial questions**

1. *Explain.* Explain why the sphenoid is called the keystone of the skull. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1887927]</small>

   Model answer:

   - It is a single bone in the centre of the skull base.
   - Almost every other bone of the skull joins with it.
   - It forms much of the central skull base and part of the sides.
   - Parts of the cranial fossae and the back of each orbit are sphenoid bone.

2. *Predict.* A pituitary tumour grows up out of the sella turcica. Predict the visual problem and explain it. <small>[OpenStax 14.2 Central Processing, ¶fs-id2365874; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1469126]</small>

   Model answer:

   - It presses on the optic chiasm just above.
   - The crossing fibres are affected; fibres to the same side are not.
   - The outer part of the visual field is lost on both sides.
   - The patient cannot see objects to their left and right.

3. *Compare.* Compare the optic canal and the superior orbital fissure. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id2026215; Atlas academic reviewer, 25 November 2026]</small>

   Model answer:

   - Optic canal: at the front outer corner of the sella turcica.
   - It carries the optic nerve.
   - Superior orbital fissure: beside the optic canal, between the lesser and greater wings of the sphenoid.
   - It carries nerves to the eye muscles and sensory nerves to the forehead.

### 1.5 Mandible

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 7.2 The Skull; 7.5; 9.6; 13.4. Status: draft.*

**1. In one line**

- The lower jaw: the only skull bone that moves (mandible). <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1891273]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* The mandible is your lower jaw bone. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1891273]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* It is one of two unpaired facial bones; the other is the vomer. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1861377]</small>

**2. Where it is**

- It forms the lower jaw, below the upper jaw bones (maxillae). <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1861377; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1891273]</small>
- At the back, each side rises to meet the temporal bone just in front of the ear canal. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1370107; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1882965]</small>
- The hyoid bone lies just below it, in the upper neck. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1399933]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* It forms your chin and lower jaw, and hinges just in front of your ears. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1891273; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1882965]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* Each condyle sits in the mandibular fossa of the temporal bone, with a disc between them. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1380884]</small>

**3. Key features**

- **Body.** The horizontal, U-shaped part that holds the lower teeth. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1891273]</small>
- **Ramus.** The upright part at the back of each side (“ramus” means branch). <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1891273]</small>
- **Angle.** The corner where the body and ramus meet. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1891273]</small>
- **Condylar process.** The back projection, topped by the condyle. It forms the jaw joint with the temporal bone. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1370107]</small>
- **Coronoid process.** The flat front projection. A biting muscle attaches to it. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1370107]</small>
- **Mandibular foramen.** An opening on the inner ramus. The nerve and vessels to the lower teeth enter here. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1327677]</small>
- **Mental foramen.** An opening at the front. A sensory nerve to the chin comes out here. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1327677]</small>
- **Alveolar process.** The top edge of the body, which anchors the lower teeth. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1327677]</small>
- **Mylohyoid line.** A ridge inside the body. The muscle forming the floor of the mouth attaches here. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1327677]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* It has a curved body for the teeth and two upright parts that reach up to the skull. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1891273]</small>
- *Explain deeper:* The condylar process forms the jaw joint; the coronoid process anchors a biting muscle. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1370107]</small>

*Go deeper:*

**4. Form → function**

- As the mouth opens, the jaw hinges down and the condyle glides forwards onto a bony bump (articular tubercle). This lets the mouth open wide. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2608267]</small>
- A flexible disc between the condyle and the temporal bone smooths the movement. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1380884]</small>
- Its processes and ridges give attachment to the strong chewing muscles. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1370107; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1399296]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* It hinges and slides at the jaw joint, so you can open wide and chew. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2608267]</small>

**5. Relations**

- *joint*, **Temporomandibular joint.** The condyle meets the mandibular fossa and articular tubercle of the temporal bone. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1380884]</small>
- *muscles*, **Chewing muscles.** Arise from the zygomatic arch and fill the temporal and infratemporal fossae. They move the mandible. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1285208; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1399296]</small>
- *nerve*, **Trigeminal nerve.** Controls the chewing muscles and gives feeling to the face. <small>[OpenStax 13.4 The Peripheral Nervous System, ¶fs-id1271098]</small>
- *nerve*, **Inferior alveolar nerve.** <mark>† The nerve to the lower teeth: enters the mandibular foramen.</mark><br><small>**Partly sourced:** OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1327677. **Not found:** OpenStax describes the nerve without naming it; “inferior alveolar nerve” needs an approved source.</small>
- *ligament*, **Ligament to the lingula.** From the base of the skull to the lingula. It steadies the jaw as the mouth opens and closes. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1327677; OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2608267]</small>
- *muscle*, **Floor of the mouth.** The muscle forming the floor of the mouth attaches to the mylohyoid lines. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1327677]</small>
- *Explain simpler:* Chewing muscles move it, and nerves to the lower teeth and chin run through it. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1399296; OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1327677]</small>

**6. Development**

- At birth, it is in two halves. They fuse during the first year into one U-shaped bone. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1891273]</small>
- In childhood the jaws grow as the teeth come in. This enlarges the face. <small>[OpenStax 7.5 Embryonic Development of the Axial Skeleton, ¶fs-id1241553]</small>
- <mark>† Like other flat bones of the face, most of it forms directly from membrane (intramembranous ossification).</mark><br><small>**Partly sourced:** OpenStax 6.4 Bone Formation and Development, ¶fs-id1804592. **Not found:** OpenStax says the flat bones of the face develop this way; that this includes most of the mandible needs an approved source.</small>

**7. Variations**

- <mark>† In males the angle of the mandible tends to be squarer and more flared.</mark><br><small>**No source found.** Not in OpenStax A&amp;P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).</small>

**8. Clinical reasoning**

1. **Injury:** Opening the mouth too wide, as in a large bite, or a blow to the jaw. **Damaged:** The jaw joint dislocates: the condyle moves in front of the articular tubercle. **Patient can’t:** Close the mouth. **Why:** The condyle is stuck in front of the tubercle and cannot slide back. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1933265]</small>
2. **Injury:** A dental injection near the mandibular foramen. **Damaged:** The sensory nerve to the lower teeth. **Patient can’t:** Feel the lower teeth on that side. **Why:** The nerve is reached before it enters the foramen and runs through the bone to the teeth. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1327677]</small>
3. **Injury:** Arthritis, grinding of the teeth or damage to the disc (temporomandibular joint disorder). **Damaged:** The jaw joint and its disc. **Patient can’t:** Chew comfortably; sometimes move the jaw at all (“lock jaw”). **Why:** The worn or damaged joint surfaces no longer glide smoothly. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1933265]</small>

**9. Tutorial questions**

1. *Explain.* Explain how the temporomandibular joint lets the mouth open wide. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1380884; OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2608267]</small>

   Model answer:

   - The mandible hinges downwards.
   - At the same time the condyle and disc glide forwards onto the articular tubercle.
   - The net movement of the condyle is forwards and downwards.
   - The disc smooths the movement between the bones.

2. *Predict.* A patient yawns widely and cannot close their mouth. Predict where the condyle is. <small>[OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id1933265; OpenStax 9.6 Anatomy of Selected Synovial Joints, ¶fs-id2608267]</small>

   Model answer:

   - The jaw joint has dislocated.
   - The condyle has moved forwards, in front of the articular tubercle.
   - It cannot slide back into the mandibular fossa.
   - So the mouth stays open.

3. *Compare.* Compare the mandibular foramen and the mental foramen. <small>[OpenStax 7.2 The Skull, ¶fs-id1327677]</small>

   Model answer:

   - Mandibular foramen: on the inner side of the ramus.
   - The nerve and vessels to the lower teeth enter it.
   - Mental foramen: at the front of the body, on each side.
   - A sensory nerve to the chin comes out through it.
