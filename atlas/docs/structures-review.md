## 1. Humerus

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb; 8.5; 9.6; 20.5 · id `humerus` · bone `humerus_left` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**The long bone of the upper arm, running from the shoulder to the elbow (humerus). <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691; 8.2 ¶fs-id1415448]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The humerus is the bone of your upper arm. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>

*Deeper.* It is a long bone: a hollow shaft (diaphysis) with a wider end at each end (epiphysis). <sub>[6.3 ¶fs-id1025647]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It sits in the arm, between the shoulder above and the elbow below. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1415448]</sub>
- Its round head faces inwards (medially) and fits the shallow socket of the shoulder blade. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- At the elbow it meets the ulna on the inner side and the radius on the outer side. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

*Simpler.* It runs from your shoulder down to your elbow. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1415448]</sub>

*Deeper.* It forms the shoulder (glenohumeral) joint above and part of the elbow joint below. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343; 8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Head.** The large, smooth, round top. It forms the shoulder joint with the glenoid cavity of the scapula. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- **Anatomical neck.** The edge of the smooth head. It marks where the joint surface ends. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- **Greater and lesser tubercles.** Two bumps near the top. The rotator cuff muscles attach to them. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691; 9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>
- **Intertubercular (bicipital) groove.** The groove between the tubercles. A tendon of biceps brachii runs in it. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- **Surgical neck.** Where the wide top narrows into the shaft. It is a common site of fracture. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- **Deltoid tuberosity.** A rough V-shaped area halfway down the outer shaft. The deltoid muscle attaches here. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- **Medial and lateral epicondyles.** Bony points either side of the elbow. Forearm muscles start from them. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1949647]</sub>
- **Trochlea and capitulum.** The pulley-shaped trochlea meets the ulna. The knob-like capitulum meets the radius. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>
- **Olecranon fossa.** A hollow at the back. It takes the tip of the ulna when the elbow straightens. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

*Simpler.* Its top end makes the shoulder joint and its bottom end makes the elbow joint. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691; 8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

*Deeper.* The bone usually breaks at the surgical neck, not the anatomical neck. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691; 8.2 ¶fs-id2030259]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- The big round head sits in a socket about a third of its size. This gives the shoulder its huge range of movement. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1231336; 9.6 ¶fs-id2305343]</sub>
- The price is stability: the shoulder relies on muscles, not bone, to hold the head in place. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343; 9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>
- The medial epicondyle is larger than the lateral one. The strong gripping muscles of the forearm pull on it. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1949647]</sub>
- Hollows above the elbow surfaces make room for the forearm bones. So the elbow can bend and straighten fully. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

*Simpler.* Its shape lets the shoulder move freely and the elbow bend and straighten fully. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343; 8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *muscle* **Rotator cuff.** Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis attach to the tubercles. They hold the head in its socket. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>
- *muscle* **Deltoid.** Attaches to the deltoid tuberosity on the outer shaft. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- *muscle* **Biceps brachii.** One of its tendons runs in the intertubercular groove. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- *artery* **Humeral circumflex arteries.** Branches of the axillary artery that supply the area near the head. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id1595346]</sub>
- *artery* **Brachial artery.** Runs down the arm and splits near the elbow into the radial and ulnar arteries. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id1595346]</sub>
- *nerves* **Four major nerves.** Four major nerves of the upper limb lie close to the humerus. Fractures can damage them. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2166864]</sub>
- *nerve* **Axillary nerve.** Winds around the surgical neck. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>
- *nerve* **Radial nerve.** Lies in the radial (spiral) groove on the back of the shaft. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>
- *nerve* **Ulnar nerve.** Passes behind the medial epicondyle. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* Muscles that move the shoulder attach near the top. Important nerves and arteries run close to the bone. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2429784; 8.2 ¶fs-id2166864]</sub>

### 6. Development

- It forms by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification), like all limb bones except the clavicle. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>
- Bone first appears in the shaft by about the twelfth week. Centres in the ends appear later, usually after birth. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>
- Growth plates between the shaft and ends let the bone lengthen. They close at adult size. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- A few people have a small hook of bone above the medial epicondyle (supracondylar process). It can press on the median nerve. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A fall onto the hand or elbow breaks the bone across the surgical neck.  
   **Damaged:** The surgical neck; the shaft can be driven into the head (impacted fracture).  
   **Patient can’t:** Move the shoulder without pain.  
   **Why:** The narrow neck is where the wide top meets the shaft, so force concentrates there. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2030259; 8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A fracture of the middle of the shaft.  
   **Damaged:** The radial nerve in the radial groove.  
   **Patient can’t:** Lift the wrist and fingers (wrist drop).  
   **Why:** The radial nerve supplies the muscles on the back of the forearm that extend the wrist and fingers. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

3. **Injury:** A child falls on an outstretched hand with the elbow straight (the commonest type). The bone breaks just above the epicondyles (supracondylar fracture).  
   **Damaged:** The artery to the forearm (brachial artery), squeezed by swelling.  
   **Patient can’t:** Keep the forearm muscles healthy: without blood they can quickly be damaged for good.  
   **Why:** The brachial artery runs down to the elbow right beside the broken bone. † <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2166864; 20.5 ¶fs-id1595346; reviewer, 2026-11-25; † Mechanism: OpenStax 8.2 describes a fall onto the tip of the elbow. The reviewer gives the commonest type, a fall on an outstretched hand with the elbow straight; this needs an approved source.]</sub>

4. **Injury:** A fall onto the hand or elbow with the arm raised fully out to the side.  
   **Damaged:** The shoulder joint: the head of the humerus usually dislocates forwards and downwards (anterior dislocation, over 95% of cases).  
   **Patient can’t:** Move the shoulder.  
   **Why:** The underside of the joint has the least support. The head is forced out below it and comes to rest in front. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1894060; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Predict.* **Predict the deficit if the radial nerve is damaged in the radial (spiral) groove, and explain why.** † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

   - Wrist drop: the patient cannot lift (extend) the wrist.
   - The fingers cannot be straightened at the knuckles.
   - The radial nerve supplies the extensor muscles on the back of the forearm.
   - Triceps is usually spared: its branches leave the nerve above the groove.
   - Numbness over the back of the hand near the thumb.

2. *Explain.* **Explain why the shoulder usually dislocates forwards and downwards (anterior dislocation).** <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1231336; 9.6 ¶fs-id1894060; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>

   - The glenoid cavity is small and shallow: about a third of the size of the head.
   - The underside of the joint has the least support.
   - Force on a fully raised arm drives the head out through the weak underside.
   - It comes to rest forwards and downwards: anterior dislocation, over 95% of cases.

3. *Compare.* **Compare the anatomical neck and the surgical neck of the humerus.** <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691; 8.2 ¶fs-id2030259]</sub>

   - Anatomical neck: the edge of the smooth joint surface of the head.
   - Surgical neck: where the wide top joins the narrow shaft.
   - The surgical neck is a common fracture site.
   - Fractures there can be impacted, with the shaft driven into the head.

## 2. Scapula

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle; 8.5; 9.6 · id `scapula` · bone `scapula_left` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**The flat, triangular shoulder blade at the back of the shoulder (scapula). <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1471081]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The scapula is your shoulder blade. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1471081]</sub>

*Deeper.* With the clavicle it forms the shoulder (pectoral) girdle, which anchors the arm to the trunk. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1371880; 8.1 ¶fs-id1968095]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It lies at the back of the shoulder. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1471081]</sub>
- Muscles lie in front of it and behind it, so it does not touch the ribs. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1968095]</sub>
- Outwards, it meets the humerus at the shoulder joint and the clavicle at the tip of the shoulder. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1471081; 8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>

*Simpler.* It sits on your upper back, behind your shoulder. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1471081]</sub>

*Deeper.* It has no joint with the ribs. Muscles cover it on both sides. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1968095]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Glenoid cavity.** The shallow socket for the head of the humerus. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1725063]</sub>
- **Spine.** The ridge across the back. With the acromion and clavicle it makes a V-shaped line for muscles. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>
- **Acromion.** The flat outer end of the spine. It is the bony tip of the shoulder and meets the clavicle. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>
- **Coracoid process.** A hook at the front shaped like a crow’s beak. It anchors chest and arm muscles and a strong ligament. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>
- **Supraspinous, infraspinous and subscapular fossae.** Broad hollows. They give large areas for muscles that move the arm. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1989073]</sub>
- **Inferior angle.** The lowest corner. Several powerful muscles of the shoulder and arm attach here. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1725063]</sub>
- **Supraglenoid and infraglenoid tubercles.** Small bumps above and below the socket, for arm muscles. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1725063]</sub>
- **Suprascapular notch.** A notch in the top border. The suprascapular nerve passes through it. † <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1725063; † OpenStax gives the notch’s position; the nerve passing through it is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* It has a shallow socket for the arm bone, a ridge across the back and two hooks at the top. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1725063; 8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>

*Deeper.* The spine, acromion and clavicle form a V-shaped line where neck and back muscles attach. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- Its socket is shallow, so the arm can move very widely. The cost is a less stable joint. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343; 9.6 ¶fs-id1231336]</sub>
- A small rim of fibrocartilage (glenoid labrum) deepens the socket slightly. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1231336]</sub>
- Its broad hollows and ridges give big areas for the muscles that move and steady the arm. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1989073; 8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>
- It is not joined to the ribs by bone. It moves with the clavicle as one unit. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1968095; 8.1 ¶fs-id1471081]</sub>

*Simpler.* Its shallow socket lets your arm move freely, and its wide surfaces hold strong muscles. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343; 8.1 ¶fs-id1989073]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *muscle* **Rotator cuff.** Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis start on the scapula and attach to the humerus. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>
- *joint* **Shoulder (glenohumeral) joint.** Ball-and-socket joint between the glenoid cavity and the head of the humerus. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343]</sub>
- *joint* **Acromioclavicular joint.** Where the acromion meets the clavicle. Its own ligaments are fairly weak. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>
- *ligament* **Coracoclavicular ligament.** A very strong band from the coracoid to the clavicle. It gives the acromioclavicular joint its main support. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>
- *bursa* **Subacromial bursa.** A fluid sac that stops the rotator cuff tendons rubbing on the scapula. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>
- *nerve* **Long thoracic nerve.** Supplies serratus anterior, which holds the scapula against the chest wall. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* The muscles that hold the shoulder joint together start on the scapula. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>

### 6. Development

- It forms by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification), like all girdle bones except the clavicle. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- In some adults part of the acromion never fuses to the rest (os acromiale). It can be mistaken for a fracture. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A strong blow to the outer shoulder, such as a player driven into the boards.  
   **Damaged:** The acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular ligaments tear (a “shoulder separation”).  
   **Patient can’t:** Hold the shoulder up: it droops.  
   **Why:** The scapula separates from the clavicle and the weight of the arm pulls it down. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>

2. **Injury:** Repeated use with the arm raised out to the side, as in throwing, swimming or racquet sports.  
   **Damaged:** The rotator cuff tendons, bursae or glenoid labrum.  
   **Patient can’t:** Raise and use the arm without pain.  
   **Why:** These tendons and the bursae become inflamed or torn with repeated use in raising the arm. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1894060]</sub>

3. **Injury:** Injury to the long thoracic nerve.  
   **Damaged:** Serratus anterior.  
   **Patient can’t:** Push against a wall without the scapula sticking out (“winging”).  
   **Why:** Serratus anterior holds the scapula flat against the chest wall. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain how the shape of the scapula trades stability for mobility at the shoulder.** <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1231336; 9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>

   - The glenoid cavity is small and shallow: about a third of the size of the humeral head.
   - The joint capsule is thin and loose.
   - The glenoid labrum deepens the socket only slightly.
   - The rotator cuff muscles give the main support, acting as “dynamic ligaments”.

2. *Predict.* **A rugby player lands on the point of the shoulder. Predict the injury and what you would see.** <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>

   - Dislocation of the acromioclavicular joint (shoulder separation).
   - Both the acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular ligaments rupture.
   - The acromion is forced under the end of the clavicle.
   - The shoulder droops as the weight of the arm pulls the scapula down.

3. *Compare.* **Compare the coracoid process and the acromion.** <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1475196; 8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>

   - Coracoid: a hook at the front that curves forwards and outwards.
   - It anchors chest and arm muscles and the coracoclavicular ligament.
   - Acromion: the flat outer end of the spine of the scapula.
   - It forms the tip of the shoulder and meets the clavicle.

## 3. Scaphoid

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb; 8.5 · id `scaphoid` · bone `scaphoid_left` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**A small boat-shaped wrist bone on the thumb side of the first row of carpal bones (scaphoid). <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The scaphoid is a small wrist bone at the base of the thumb. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414]</sub>

*Deeper.* The scaphoid and lunate meet the radius at the wrist joint; the triquetrum meets the articular disc over the ulna. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It is the outermost bone of the first (proximal) row of carpal bones, on the thumb side. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414]</sub>
- Above it is the radius. Beside it, on the inner side, is the lunate. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; 8.2 ¶fs-id1977414]</sub>
- Below it are the trapezium, trapezoid and capitate of the second row. <sub>[glossary: scaphoid]</sub>

*Simpler.* It sits in your wrist, just below the end of the forearm, on the thumb side. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414; 8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>

*Deeper.* With the lunate it meets the radius directly. The triquetrum, beside them, meets the articular disc over the ulna. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Boat shape.** “Scaphoid” means boat-shaped. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414]</sub>
- **Surface for the radius.** It meets the end of the radius directly at the wrist joint. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>
- **Front (palmar) surface.** It anchors the flexor retinaculum, the band that roofs the carpal tunnel. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2329218]</sub>
- **Waist.** The narrow middle part. Most fractures happen here. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* It is small and boat-shaped, and it sits right against the end of the radius. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414; 8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>

*Deeper.* With the other carpal bones it forms the walls and floor of the carpal tunnel. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2329218]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- It sits on the thumb side and meets the radius directly. So a fall on the outstretched hand drives force through it. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; 8.2 ¶fs-id1989621]</sub>
- At the wrist (radiocarpal) joint, the scaphoid and lunate meet the radius. The triquetrum meets the articular disc over the ulna. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>
- With the second row it forms the midcarpal joint. These two joints make all the movements of the wrist. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id932152]</sub>

*Simpler.* It helps the wrist bend and passes force from the hand to the forearm. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id932152; 8.2 ¶fs-id1989621]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *joint* **Wrist (radiocarpal) joint.** The scaphoid and lunate meet the radius here; the triquetrum meets the articular disc over the ulna. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>
- *joint* **Midcarpal joint.** The joint between the first and second rows of carpal bones. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id932152]</sub>
- *ligament* **Flexor retinaculum.** Attached on the thumb side to the trapezium and scaphoid. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2329218]</sub>
- *artery* **Radial artery.** Its branches enter mainly at the far (distal) end of the scaphoid. † <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id1595346; † OpenStax traces the radial artery to the wrist; where its branches enter the scaphoid is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>
- *space* **Anatomical snuffbox.** It lies in the floor of this hollow at the base of the thumb. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* It forms joints with the forearm and the other wrist bones. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; 8.2 ¶fs-id932152]</sub>

### 6. Development

- It forms by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification), like all limb bones except the clavicle. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>
- Its ossification centre appears in early childhood, at about five years. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 7. Variations

*None from an approved source yet; the app says so.*

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A fall onto the outstretched hand.  
   **Damaged:** The scaphoid: the carpal bone fractured most often.  
   **Patient can’t:** Use the wrist without deep pain on the thumb side.  
   **Why:** Force passes from the hand to the radius through the scaphoid. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2637051; 8.2 ¶fs-id1989621; 8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A scaphoid fracture is missed because the first X-ray looks normal.  
   **Damaged:** The bone itself, which has a poor blood supply.  
   **Patient can’t:** Heal quickly: part of the bone can die (necrosis), leading later to arthritis of the wrist.  
   **Why:** With little blood reaching it, the broken bone heals slowly. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2637051]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain why a scaphoid fracture can be missed, and why that matters.** <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2637051]</sub>

   - Deep pain at the outer wrist can be taken for a sprain.
   - The fracture may not show on an X-ray until weeks later, once swelling settles.
   - Its blood supply is poor, so it heals slowly.
   - Untreated, part of the bone can die, leading to arthritis of the wrist.

2. *Predict.* **Predict which part of the scaphoid dies after a fracture across its waist, and explain why.** † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

   - The near (proximal) part.
   - Blood vessels enter mainly at the far (distal) end.
   - A break across the waist cuts the near part off from its supply.
   - Without blood, it dies (avascular necrosis).

3. *Compare.* **Compare a scaphoid fracture with a Colles fracture after a fall on the outstretched hand.** <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2637051; 8.2 ¶fs-id2142217]</sub>

   - Both follow a fall onto the outstretched hand.
   - Scaphoid: deep pain at the outer wrist; may not show on the first X-ray.
   - Colles: the end of the radius breaks and is pushed backwards and upwards.
   - Colles gives a “dinner fork” bend just above the wrist.

## 4. Sphenoid bone

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 7.2 The Skull; 6.4; 14.2 · id `sphenoid` · bone `sphenoid_bone` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**A single, complex bone in the centre of the skull base that joins almost every other skull bone (sphenoid bone). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The sphenoid is a winged bone in the middle of the floor of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>

*Deeper.* It is one of the eight bones of the brain case. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1351960]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It forms much of the floor of the middle of the skull base. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- Its outer parts reach the sides of the skull, just in front of the temporal bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- Behind each eye, it forms the back wall of the eye socket (orbit). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

*Simpler.* The sphenoid sits deep in the middle of your head, behind your eyes. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126; 7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

*Deeper.* Because it joins with almost every other bone of the skull, it is called a “keystone” bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Lesser wings.** Form the ridge between the front and middle hollows (fossae) of the skull floor. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- **Greater wings.** Form the front of the middle fossa and part of the side of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- **Sella turcica.** A saddle-shaped seat in the midline. Its hollow (hypophyseal fossa) holds the pituitary gland. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- **Optic canal.** Beside the sella turcica. It carries the optic nerve from the eye to the brain. <sub>[glossary: optic canal; 7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>
- **Superior orbital fissure.** A slit between the lesser and greater wings of the sphenoid, into the back of the orbit. It carries nerves to the eye muscles and the forehead. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>
- **Foramen rotundum, foramen ovale and foramen spinosum.** Openings in the floor of the middle fossa for nerves to the face and an artery to the brain’s coverings. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; † OpenStax places these in the floor of the middle cranial fossa; that they lie in the sphenoid’s greater wing is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>
- **Medial and lateral pterygoid plates.** Thin wing-shaped plates underneath. The lateral plates anchor chewing muscles. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1355530]</sub>
- **Sphenoid sinus.** An air-filled space in its body: the most posterior paranasal sinus. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1882870]</sub>

*Simpler.* It has wings, a saddle that holds the pituitary gland and holes for nerves to the eye and face. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126; 7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>

*Deeper.* Its lesser wings mark the border between the front (anterior) and middle cranial fossae. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- Its central position lets it act as a keystone, linking almost every other bone of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- Nerves and vessels pass through its openings between the brain and the eye, face and jaws. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>
- Its air-filled sinus lightens the skull and adds resonance to the voice. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977054; 7.2 ¶fs-id1882870]</sub>

*Simpler.* It locks the skull bones together and lets nerves pass through to the face. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126; 7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *gland* **Pituitary gland.** Sits in the hypophyseal fossa of the sella turcica. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- *nerve* **Optic nerve.** Passes through the optic canal. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>
- *nerves* **Nerves to the eye muscles.** Pass through the superior orbital fissure. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>
- *nerve* **Maxillary nerve.** Leaves through the foramen rotundum to supply the cheek, nose and upper teeth. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; † OpenStax describes the nerve without naming it; “maxillary nerve” needs an approved source.]</sub>
- *artery* **Middle meningeal artery.** Enters through the foramen spinosum to supply the coverings of the brain. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; † OpenStax describes the artery without naming it; “middle meningeal artery” needs an approved source.]</sub>
- *muscles* **Chewing muscles.** Attach to the lateral pterygoid plates. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1355530]</sub>
- *bones* **Pterion.** Its greater wing is one of four bones meeting at the pterion, the thinnest part of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

*Simpler.* The pituitary gland sits on it, and nerves to the eye and face pass through it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126; 7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>

### 6. Development

- Bones of the skull base form by replacing cartilage with bone (endochondral ossification). <sub>[6.4 ¶fs-id1966864]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- The size of the sphenoid sinus varies a great deal from person to person. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A tumour of the pituitary gland grows upwards out of the sella turcica.  
   **Damaged:** The optic chiasm, where nerve fibres from the two eyes cross.  
   **Patient can’t:** See objects to the right and left: the outer parts of both visual fields are lost.  
   **Why:** The crossing fibres sit just above the pituitary gland and are pressed on first. <sub>[14.2 ¶fs-id2365874; 7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A hard blow to the side of the head at the pterion.  
   **Damaged:** The artery just inside the skull there; blood collects and presses on the brain.  
   **Patient can’t:** Stay well: the patient may seem fine at first, then worsen and can die within hours without treatment.  
   **Why:** The pterion is the thinnest part of the skull, with a major artery right under it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1364278; 7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain why the sphenoid is called the keystone of the skull.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126; 7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

   - It is a single bone in the centre of the skull base.
   - Almost every other bone of the skull joins with it.
   - It forms much of the central skull base and part of the sides.
   - Parts of the cranial fossae and the back of each orbit are sphenoid bone.

2. *Predict.* **A pituitary tumour grows up out of the sella turcica. Predict the visual problem and explain it.** <sub>[14.2 ¶fs-id2365874; 7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>

   - It presses on the optic chiasm just above.
   - The crossing fibres are affected; fibres to the same side are not.
   - The outer part of the visual field is lost on both sides.
   - The patient cannot see objects to their left and right.

3. *Compare.* **Compare the optic canal and the superior orbital fissure.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>

   - Optic canal: at the front outer corner of the sella turcica.
   - It carries the optic nerve.
   - Superior orbital fissure: beside the optic canal, between the lesser and greater wings of the sphenoid.
   - It carries nerves to the eye muscles and sensory nerves to the forehead.

## 5. Mandible

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 7.2 The Skull; 7.5; 9.6; 13.4 · id `mandible` · bone `mandible` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**The lower jaw: the only skull bone that moves (mandible). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The mandible is your lower jaw bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>

*Deeper.* It is one of two unpaired facial bones; the other is the vomer. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1861377]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It forms the lower jaw, below the upper jaw bones (maxillae). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1861377; 7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>
- At the back, each side rises to meet the temporal bone just in front of the ear canal. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1370107; 7.2 ¶fs-id1882965]</sub>
- The hyoid bone lies just below it, in the upper neck. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1399933]</sub>

*Simpler.* It forms your chin and lower jaw, and hinges just in front of your ears. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273; 7.2 ¶fs-id1882965]</sub>

*Deeper.* Each condyle sits in the mandibular fossa of the temporal bone, with a disc between them. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1380884]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Body.** The horizontal, U-shaped part that holds the lower teeth. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>
- **Ramus.** The upright part at the back of each side (“ramus” means branch). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>
- **Angle.** The corner where the body and ramus meet. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>
- **Condylar process.** The back projection, topped by the condyle. It forms the jaw joint with the temporal bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1370107]</sub>
- **Coronoid process.** The flat front projection. A biting muscle attaches to it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1370107]</sub>
- **Mandibular foramen.** An opening on the inner ramus. The nerve and vessels to the lower teeth enter here. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>
- **Mental foramen.** An opening at the front. A sensory nerve to the chin comes out here. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>
- **Alveolar process.** The top edge of the body, which anchors the lower teeth. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>
- **Mylohyoid line.** A ridge inside the body. The muscle forming the floor of the mouth attaches here. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>

*Simpler.* It has a curved body for the teeth and two upright parts that reach up to the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>

*Deeper.* The condylar process forms the jaw joint; the coronoid process anchors a biting muscle. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1370107]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- As the mouth opens, the jaw hinges down and the condyle glides forwards onto a bony bump (articular tubercle). This lets the mouth open wide. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2608267]</sub>
- A flexible disc between the condyle and the temporal bone smooths the movement. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1380884]</sub>
- Its processes and ridges give attachment to the strong chewing muscles. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1370107; 7.2 ¶fs-id1399296]</sub>

*Simpler.* It hinges and slides at the jaw joint, so you can open wide and chew. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2608267]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *joint* **Temporomandibular joint.** The condyle meets the mandibular fossa and articular tubercle of the temporal bone. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1380884]</sub>
- *muscles* **Chewing muscles.** Arise from the zygomatic arch and fill the temporal and infratemporal fossae. They move the mandible. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1285208; 7.2 ¶fs-id1399296]</sub>
- *nerve* **Trigeminal nerve.** Controls the chewing muscles and gives feeling to the face. <sub>[13.4 ¶fs-id1271098]</sub>
- *nerve* **Inferior alveolar nerve.** The nerve to the lower teeth: enters the mandibular foramen. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677; † OpenStax describes the nerve without naming it; “inferior alveolar nerve” needs an approved source.]</sub>
- *ligament* **Ligament to the lingula.** From the base of the skull to the lingula. It steadies the jaw as the mouth opens and closes. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677; 9.6 ¶fs-id2608267]</sub>
- *muscle* **Floor of the mouth.** The muscle forming the floor of the mouth attaches to the mylohyoid lines. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>

*Simpler.* Chewing muscles move it, and nerves to the lower teeth and chin run through it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1399296; 7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>

### 6. Development

- At birth, it is in two halves. They fuse during the first year into one U-shaped bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>
- In childhood the jaws grow as the teeth come in. This enlarges the face. <sub>[7.5 ¶fs-id1241553]</sub>
- Like other flat bones of the face, most of it forms directly from membrane (intramembranous ossification). † <sub>[6.4 ¶fs-id1804592; † OpenStax says the flat bones of the face develop this way; that this includes most of the mandible needs an approved source.]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- In males the angle of the mandible tends to be squarer and more flared. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** Opening the mouth too wide, as in a large bite, or a blow to the jaw.  
   **Damaged:** The jaw joint dislocates: the condyle moves in front of the articular tubercle.  
   **Patient can’t:** Close the mouth.  
   **Why:** The condyle is stuck in front of the tubercle and cannot slide back. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1933265]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A dental injection near the mandibular foramen.  
   **Damaged:** The sensory nerve to the lower teeth.  
   **Patient can’t:** Feel the lower teeth on that side.  
   **Why:** The nerve is reached before it enters the foramen and runs through the bone to the teeth. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>

3. **Injury:** Arthritis, grinding of the teeth or damage to the disc (temporomandibular joint disorder).  
   **Damaged:** The jaw joint and its disc.  
   **Patient can’t:** Chew comfortably; sometimes move the jaw at all (“lock jaw”).  
   **Why:** The worn or damaged joint surfaces no longer glide smoothly. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1933265]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain how the temporomandibular joint lets the mouth open wide.** <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1380884; 9.6 ¶fs-id2608267]</sub>

   - The mandible hinges downwards.
   - At the same time the condyle and disc glide forwards onto the articular tubercle.
   - The net movement of the condyle is forwards and downwards.
   - The disc smooths the movement between the bones.

2. *Predict.* **A patient yawns widely and cannot close their mouth. Predict where the condyle is.** <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1933265; 9.6 ¶fs-id2608267]</sub>

   - The jaw joint has dislocated.
   - The condyle has moved forwards, in front of the articular tubercle.
   - It cannot slide back into the mandibular fossa.
   - So the mouth stays open.

3. *Compare.* **Compare the mandibular foramen and the mental foramen.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>

   - Mandibular foramen: on the inner side of the ramus.
   - The nerve and vessels to the lower teeth enter it.
   - Mental foramen: at the front of the body, on each side.
   - A sensory nerve to the chin comes out through it.
