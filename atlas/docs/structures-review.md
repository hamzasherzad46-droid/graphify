## 1. Humerus

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb; 8.5; 9.6; 20.5 · id `humerus` · bone `humerus_left` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**The long bone of the upper arm, running from the shoulder to the elbow (humerus). <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691; 8.2 ¶fs-id1415448]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The humerus is the bone of your upper arm. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>

*Deeper.* It is a long bone: a hollow shaft (diaphysis) with a wider end at each end (epiphysis). <sub>[6.3 ¶fs-id1025647]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It sits in the arm, between the shoulder above and the elbow below. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1415448]</sub>
- Its round head faces inwards (medially) and fits the shallow socket of the shoulder blade. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- At the elbow it meets the ulna on the inner side and the radius on the outer side. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

*Simpler.* It runs from your shoulder down to your elbow. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1415448]</sub>

*Deeper.* It forms the shoulder (glenohumeral) joint above and part of the elbow joint below. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343; 8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Head.** The large, smooth, round top. It forms the shoulder joint with the glenoid cavity of the scapula. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- **Anatomical neck.** The edge of the smooth head. It marks where the joint surface ends. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- **Greater and lesser tubercles.** Two bumps near the top. The rotator cuff muscles attach to them. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691; 9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>
- **Intertubercular (bicipital) groove.** The groove between the tubercles. A tendon of biceps brachii runs in it. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- **Surgical neck.** Where the wide top narrows into the shaft. It is a common site of fracture. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- **Deltoid tuberosity.** A rough V-shaped area halfway down the outer shaft. The deltoid muscle attaches here. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- **Medial and lateral epicondyles.** Bony points either side of the elbow. Forearm muscles start from them. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1949647]</sub>
- **Trochlea and capitulum.** The pulley-shaped trochlea meets the ulna. The knob-like capitulum meets the radius. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>
- **Olecranon fossa.** A hollow at the back. It takes the tip of the ulna when the elbow straightens. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

*Simpler.* Its top end makes the shoulder joint and its bottom end makes the elbow joint. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691; 8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

*Deeper.* The bone usually breaks at the surgical neck, not the anatomical neck. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691; 8.2 ¶fs-id2030259]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- The big round head sits in a socket about a third of its size. This gives the shoulder its huge range of movement. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1231336; 9.6 ¶fs-id2305343]</sub>
- The price is stability: the shoulder relies on muscles, not bone, to hold the head in place. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343; 9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>
- The medial epicondyle is larger than the lateral one. The strong gripping muscles of the forearm pull on it. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1949647]</sub>
- Hollows above the elbow surfaces make room for the forearm bones. So the elbow can bend and straighten fully. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

*Simpler.* Its shape lets the shoulder move freely and the elbow bend and straighten fully. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343; 8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *muscle* **Rotator cuff.** Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis attach to the tubercles. They hold the head in its socket. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>
- *muscle* **Deltoid.** Attaches to the deltoid tuberosity on the outer shaft. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- *muscle* **Biceps brachii.** One of its tendons runs in the intertubercular groove. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>
- *artery* **Humeral circumflex arteries.** Branches of the axillary artery that supply the area near the head. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id1595346]</sub>
- *artery* **Brachial artery.** Runs down the arm and splits near the elbow into the radial and ulnar arteries. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id1595346]</sub>
- *nerves* **Four major nerves.** Four major nerves of the upper limb lie close to the humerus. Fractures can damage them. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2166864]</sub>
- *nerve* **Axillary nerve.** Winds around the surgical neck. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>
- *nerve* **Radial nerve.** Lies in the radial (spiral) groove on the back of the shaft. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>
- *nerve* **Ulnar nerve.** Passes behind the medial epicondyle. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* Muscles that move the shoulder attach near the top. Important nerves and arteries run close to the bone. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2429784; 8.2 ¶fs-id2166864]</sub>

### 6. Development

- It forms by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification), like all limb bones except the clavicle. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>
- Bone first appears in the shaft by about the twelfth week. Centres in the ends appear later, usually after birth. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>
- Growth plates between the shaft and ends let the bone lengthen. They close at adult size. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- A few people have a small hook of bone above the medial epicondyle (supracondylar process). It can press on the median nerve. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A fall onto the hand or elbow breaks the bone across the surgical neck.  
   **Damaged:** The surgical neck; the shaft can be driven into the head (impacted fracture).  
   **Patient can’t:** Move the shoulder without pain.  
   **Why:** The narrow neck is where the wide top meets the shaft, so force concentrates there. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2030259; 8.2 ¶fs-id2110691]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A fracture of the middle of the shaft.  
   **Damaged:** The radial nerve in the radial groove.  
   **Patient can’t:** Lift the wrist and fingers (wrist drop).  
   **Why:** The radial nerve supplies the muscles on the back of the forearm that extend the wrist and fingers. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

3. **Injury:** A child falls on an outstretched hand with the elbow straight (the commonest type). The bone breaks just above the epicondyles (supracondylar fracture).  
   **Damaged:** The artery to the forearm (brachial artery), squeezed by swelling.  
   **Patient can’t:** Keep the forearm muscles healthy: without blood they can quickly be damaged for good.  
   **Why:** The brachial artery runs down to the elbow right beside the broken bone. † <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2166864; 20.5 ¶fs-id1595346; reviewer, 2026-11-25; † Mechanism: OpenStax 8.2 describes a fall onto the tip of the elbow. The reviewer gives the commonest type, a fall on an outstretched hand with the elbow straight; this needs an approved source.]</sub>

4. **Injury:** A fall onto the hand or elbow with the arm raised fully out to the side.  
   **Damaged:** The shoulder joint: the head of the humerus usually dislocates forwards and downwards (anterior dislocation, over 95% of cases).  
   **Patient can’t:** Move the shoulder.  
   **Why:** The underside of the joint has the least support. The head is forced out below it and comes to rest in front. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1894060; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Predict.* **Predict the deficit if the radial nerve is damaged in the radial (spiral) groove, and explain why.** † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

   - Wrist drop: the patient cannot lift (extend) the wrist.
   - The fingers cannot be straightened at the knuckles.
   - The radial nerve supplies the extensor muscles on the back of the forearm.
   - Triceps is usually spared: its branches leave the nerve above the groove.
   - Numbness over the back of the hand near the thumb.

2. *Explain.* **Explain why the shoulder usually dislocates forwards and downwards (anterior dislocation).** <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1231336; 9.6 ¶fs-id1894060; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>

   - The glenoid cavity is small and shallow: about a third of the size of the head.
   - The underside of the joint has the least support.
   - Force on a fully raised arm drives the head out through the weak underside.
   - It comes to rest forwards and downwards: anterior dislocation, over 95% of cases.

3. *Compare.* **Compare the anatomical neck and the surgical neck of the humerus.** <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2110691; 8.2 ¶fs-id2030259]</sub>

   - Anatomical neck: the edge of the smooth joint surface of the head.
   - Surgical neck: where the wide top joins the narrow shaft.
   - The surgical neck is a common fracture site.
   - Fractures there can be impacted, with the shaft driven into the head.

## 2. Scapula

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle; 8.5; 9.6 · id `scapula` · bone `scapula_left` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**The flat, triangular shoulder blade at the back of the shoulder (scapula). <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1471081]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The scapula is your shoulder blade. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1471081]</sub>

*Deeper.* With the clavicle it forms the shoulder (pectoral) girdle, which anchors the arm to the trunk. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1371880; 8.1 ¶fs-id1968095]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It lies at the back of the shoulder. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1471081]</sub>
- Muscles lie in front of it and behind it, so it does not touch the ribs. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1968095]</sub>
- Outwards, it meets the humerus at the shoulder joint and the clavicle at the tip of the shoulder. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1471081; 8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>

*Simpler.* It sits on your upper back, behind your shoulder. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1471081]</sub>

*Deeper.* It has no joint with the ribs. Muscles cover it on both sides. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1968095]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Glenoid cavity.** The shallow socket for the head of the humerus. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1725063]</sub>
- **Spine.** The ridge across the back. With the acromion and clavicle it makes a V-shaped line for muscles. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>
- **Acromion.** The flat outer end of the spine. It is the bony tip of the shoulder and meets the clavicle. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>
- **Coracoid process.** A hook at the front shaped like a crow’s beak. It anchors chest and arm muscles and a strong ligament. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>
- **Supraspinous, infraspinous and subscapular fossae.** Broad hollows. They give large areas for muscles that move the arm. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1989073]</sub>
- **Inferior angle.** The lowest corner. Several powerful muscles of the shoulder and arm attach here. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1725063]</sub>
- **Supraglenoid and infraglenoid tubercles.** Small bumps above and below the socket, for arm muscles. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1725063]</sub>
- **Suprascapular notch.** A notch in the top border. The suprascapular nerve passes through it. † <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1725063; † OpenStax gives the notch’s position; the nerve passing through it is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* It has a shallow socket for the arm bone, a ridge across the back and two hooks at the top. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1725063; 8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>

*Deeper.* The spine, acromion and clavicle form a V-shaped line where neck and back muscles attach. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- Its socket is shallow, so the arm can move very widely. The cost is a less stable joint. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343; 9.6 ¶fs-id1231336]</sub>
- A small rim of fibrocartilage (glenoid labrum) deepens the socket slightly. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1231336]</sub>
- Its broad hollows and ridges give big areas for the muscles that move and steady the arm. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1989073; 8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>
- It is not joined to the ribs by bone. It moves with the clavicle as one unit. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1968095; 8.1 ¶fs-id1471081]</sub>

*Simpler.* Its shallow socket lets your arm move freely, and its wide surfaces hold strong muscles. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343; 8.1 ¶fs-id1989073]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *muscle* **Rotator cuff.** Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis start on the scapula and attach to the humerus. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>
- *joint* **Shoulder (glenohumeral) joint.** Ball-and-socket joint between the glenoid cavity and the head of the humerus. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2305343]</sub>
- *joint* **Acromioclavicular joint.** Where the acromion meets the clavicle. Its own ligaments are fairly weak. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>
- *ligament* **Coracoclavicular ligament.** A very strong band from the coracoid to the clavicle. It gives the acromioclavicular joint its main support. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>
- *bursa* **Subacromial bursa.** A fluid sac that stops the rotator cuff tendons rubbing on the scapula. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>
- *nerve* **Long thoracic nerve.** Supplies serratus anterior, which holds the scapula against the chest wall. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* The muscles that hold the shoulder joint together start on the scapula. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>

### 6. Development

- It forms by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification), like all girdle bones except the clavicle. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- In some adults part of the acromion never fuses to the rest (os acromiale). It can be mistaken for a fracture. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A strong blow to the outer shoulder, such as a player driven into the boards.  
   **Damaged:** The acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular ligaments tear (a “shoulder separation”).  
   **Patient can’t:** Hold the shoulder up: it droops.  
   **Why:** The scapula separates from the clavicle and the weight of the arm pulls it down. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>

2. **Injury:** Repeated use with the arm raised out to the side, as in throwing, swimming or racquet sports.  
   **Damaged:** The rotator cuff tendons, bursae or glenoid labrum.  
   **Patient can’t:** Raise and use the arm without pain.  
   **Why:** These tendons and the bursae become inflamed or torn with repeated use in raising the arm. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1894060]</sub>

3. **Injury:** Injury to the long thoracic nerve.  
   **Damaged:** Serratus anterior.  
   **Patient can’t:** Push against a wall without the scapula sticking out (“winging”).  
   **Why:** Serratus anterior holds the scapula flat against the chest wall. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain how the shape of the scapula trades stability for mobility at the shoulder.** <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1231336; 9.6 ¶fs-id2429784]</sub>

   - The glenoid cavity is small and shallow: about a third of the size of the humeral head.
   - The joint capsule is thin and loose.
   - The glenoid labrum deepens the socket only slightly.
   - The rotator cuff muscles give the main support, acting as “dynamic ligaments”.

2. *Predict.* **A rugby player lands on the point of the shoulder. Predict the injury and what you would see.** <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>

   - Dislocation of the acromioclavicular joint (shoulder separation).
   - Both the acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular ligaments rupture.
   - The acromion is forced under the end of the clavicle.
   - The shoulder droops as the weight of the arm pulls the scapula down.

3. *Compare.* **Compare the coracoid process and the acromion.** <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1475196; 8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>

   - Coracoid: a hook at the front that curves forwards and outwards.
   - It anchors chest and arm muscles and the coracoclavicular ligament.
   - Acromion: the flat outer end of the spine of the scapula.
   - It forms the tip of the shoulder and meets the clavicle.

## 3. Scaphoid

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb; 8.5 · id `scaphoid` · bone `scaphoid_left` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**A small boat-shaped wrist bone on the thumb side of the first row of carpal bones (scaphoid). <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The scaphoid is a small wrist bone at the base of the thumb. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414]</sub>

*Deeper.* The scaphoid and lunate meet the radius at the wrist joint; the triquetrum meets the articular disc over the ulna. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It is the outermost bone of the first (proximal) row of carpal bones, on the thumb side. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414]</sub>
- Above it is the radius. Beside it, on the inner side, is the lunate. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; 8.2 ¶fs-id1977414]</sub>
- Below it are the trapezium, trapezoid and capitate of the second row. <sub>[glossary: scaphoid]</sub>

*Simpler.* It sits in your wrist, just below the end of the forearm, on the thumb side. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414; 8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>

*Deeper.* With the lunate it meets the radius directly. The triquetrum, beside them, meets the articular disc over the ulna. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Boat shape.** “Scaphoid” means boat-shaped. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414]</sub>
- **Surface for the radius.** It meets the end of the radius directly at the wrist joint. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>
- **Front (palmar) surface.** It anchors the flexor retinaculum, the band that roofs the carpal tunnel. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2329218]</sub>
- **Waist.** The narrow middle part. Most fractures happen here. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* It is small and boat-shaped, and it sits right against the end of the radius. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1977414; 8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>

*Deeper.* With the other carpal bones it forms the walls and floor of the carpal tunnel. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2329218]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- It sits on the thumb side and meets the radius directly. So a fall on the outstretched hand drives force through it. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; 8.2 ¶fs-id1989621]</sub>
- At the wrist (radiocarpal) joint, the scaphoid and lunate meet the radius. The triquetrum meets the articular disc over the ulna. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>
- With the second row it forms the midcarpal joint. These two joints make all the movements of the wrist. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id932152]</sub>

*Simpler.* It helps the wrist bend and passes force from the hand to the forearm. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id932152; 8.2 ¶fs-id1989621]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *joint* **Wrist (radiocarpal) joint.** The scaphoid and lunate meet the radius here; the triquetrum meets the articular disc over the ulna. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>
- *joint* **Midcarpal joint.** The joint between the first and second rows of carpal bones. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id932152]</sub>
- *ligament* **Flexor retinaculum.** Attached on the thumb side to the trapezium and scaphoid. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2329218]</sub>
- *artery* **Radial artery.** Its branches enter mainly at the far (distal) end of the scaphoid. † <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id1595346; † OpenStax traces the radial artery to the wrist; where its branches enter the scaphoid is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>
- *space* **Anatomical snuffbox.** It lies in the floor of this hollow at the base of the thumb. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* It forms joints with the forearm and the other wrist bones. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937; 8.2 ¶fs-id932152]</sub>

### 6. Development

- It forms by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification), like all limb bones except the clavicle. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>
- Its ossification centre appears in early childhood, at about five years. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 7. Variations

*None from an approved source yet; the app says so.*

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A fall onto the outstretched hand.  
   **Damaged:** The scaphoid: the carpal bone fractured most often.  
   **Patient can’t:** Use the wrist without deep pain on the thumb side.  
   **Why:** Force passes from the hand to the radius through the scaphoid. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2637051; 8.2 ¶fs-id1989621; 8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A scaphoid fracture is missed because the first X-ray looks normal.  
   **Damaged:** The bone itself, which has a poor blood supply.  
   **Patient can’t:** Heal quickly: part of the bone can die (necrosis), leading later to arthritis of the wrist.  
   **Why:** With little blood reaching it, the broken bone heals slowly. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2637051]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain why a scaphoid fracture can be missed, and why that matters.** <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2637051]</sub>

   - Deep pain at the outer wrist can be taken for a sprain.
   - The fracture may not show on an X-ray until weeks later, once swelling settles.
   - Its blood supply is poor, so it heals slowly.
   - Untreated, part of the bone can die, leading to arthritis of the wrist.

2. *Predict.* **Predict which part of the scaphoid dies after a fracture across its waist, and explain why.** † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

   - The near (proximal) part.
   - Blood vessels enter mainly at the far (distal) end.
   - A break across the waist cuts the near part off from its supply.
   - Without blood, it dies (avascular necrosis).

3. *Compare.* **Compare a scaphoid fracture with a Colles fracture after a fall on the outstretched hand.** <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2637051; 8.2 ¶fs-id2142217]</sub>

   - Both follow a fall onto the outstretched hand.
   - Scaphoid: deep pain at the outer wrist; may not show on the first X-ray.
   - Colles: the end of the radius breaks and is pushed backwards and upwards.
   - Colles gives a “dinner fork” bend just above the wrist.

## 4. Sphenoid bone

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 7.2 The Skull; 6.4; 14.2 · id `sphenoid` · bone `sphenoid_bone` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**A single, complex bone in the centre of the skull base that joins almost every other skull bone (sphenoid bone). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The sphenoid is a winged bone in the middle of the floor of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>

*Deeper.* It is one of the eight bones of the brain case. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1351960]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It forms much of the floor of the middle of the skull base. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- Its outer parts reach the sides of the skull, just in front of the temporal bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- Behind each eye, it forms the back wall of the eye socket (orbit). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

*Simpler.* The sphenoid sits deep in the middle of your head, behind your eyes. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126; 7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

*Deeper.* Because it joins with almost every other bone of the skull, it is called a “keystone” bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Lesser wings.** Form the ridge between the front and middle hollows (fossae) of the skull floor. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- **Greater wings.** Form the front of the middle fossa and part of the side of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- **Sella turcica.** A saddle-shaped seat in the midline. Its hollow (hypophyseal fossa) holds the pituitary gland. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- **Optic canal.** Beside the sella turcica. It carries the optic nerve from the eye to the brain. <sub>[glossary: optic canal; 7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>
- **Superior orbital fissure.** A slit between the lesser and greater wings of the sphenoid, into the back of the orbit. It carries nerves to the eye muscles and the forehead. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>
- **Foramen rotundum, foramen ovale and foramen spinosum.** Openings in the floor of the middle fossa for nerves to the face and an artery to the brain’s coverings. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; † OpenStax places these in the floor of the middle cranial fossa; that they lie in the sphenoid’s greater wing is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>
- **Medial and lateral pterygoid plates.** Thin wing-shaped plates underneath. The lateral plates anchor chewing muscles. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1355530]</sub>
- **Sphenoid sinus.** An air-filled space in its body: the most posterior paranasal sinus. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1882870]</sub>

*Simpler.* It has wings, a saddle that holds the pituitary gland and holes for nerves to the eye and face. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126; 7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>

*Deeper.* Its lesser wings mark the border between the front (anterior) and middle cranial fossae. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- Its central position lets it act as a keystone, linking almost every other bone of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- Nerves and vessels pass through its openings between the brain and the eye, face and jaws. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>
- Its air-filled sinus lightens the skull and adds resonance to the voice. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977054; 7.2 ¶fs-id1882870]</sub>

*Simpler.* It locks the skull bones together and lets nerves pass through to the face. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126; 7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *gland* **Pituitary gland.** Sits in the hypophyseal fossa of the sella turcica. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>
- *nerve* **Optic nerve.** Passes through the optic canal. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>
- *nerves* **Nerves to the eye muscles.** Pass through the superior orbital fissure. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>
- *nerve* **Maxillary nerve.** Leaves through the foramen rotundum to supply the cheek, nose and upper teeth. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; † OpenStax describes the nerve without naming it; “maxillary nerve” needs an approved source.]</sub>
- *artery* **Middle meningeal artery.** Enters through the foramen spinosum to supply the coverings of the brain. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; † OpenStax describes the artery without naming it; “middle meningeal artery” needs an approved source.]</sub>
- *muscles* **Chewing muscles.** Attach to the lateral pterygoid plates. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1355530]</sub>
- *bones* **Pterion.** Its greater wing is one of four bones meeting at the pterion, the thinnest part of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

*Simpler.* The pituitary gland sits on it, and nerves to the eye and face pass through it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126; 7.2 ¶fs-id2026215]</sub>

### 6. Development

- Bones of the skull base form by replacing cartilage with bone (endochondral ossification). <sub>[6.4 ¶fs-id1966864]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- The size of the sphenoid sinus varies a great deal from person to person. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A tumour of the pituitary gland grows upwards out of the sella turcica.  
   **Damaged:** The optic chiasm, where nerve fibres from the two eyes cross.  
   **Patient can’t:** See objects to the right and left: the outer parts of both visual fields are lost.  
   **Why:** The crossing fibres sit just above the pituitary gland and are pressed on first. <sub>[14.2 ¶fs-id2365874; 7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A hard blow to the side of the head at the pterion.  
   **Damaged:** The artery just inside the skull there; blood collects and presses on the brain.  
   **Patient can’t:** Stay well: the patient may seem fine at first, then worsen and can die within hours without treatment.  
   **Why:** The pterion is the thinnest part of the skull, with a major artery right under it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1364278; 7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain why the sphenoid is called the keystone of the skull.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1469126; 7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

   - It is a single bone in the centre of the skull base.
   - Almost every other bone of the skull joins with it.
   - It forms much of the central skull base and part of the sides.
   - Parts of the cranial fossae and the back of each orbit are sphenoid bone.

2. *Predict.* **A pituitary tumour grows up out of the sella turcica. Predict the visual problem and explain it.** <sub>[14.2 ¶fs-id2365874; 7.2 ¶fs-id1469126]</sub>

   - It presses on the optic chiasm just above.
   - The crossing fibres are affected; fibres to the same side are not.
   - The outer part of the visual field is lost on both sides.
   - The patient cannot see objects to their left and right.

3. *Compare.* **Compare the optic canal and the superior orbital fissure.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; reviewer, 2026-11-25]</sub>

   - Optic canal: at the front outer corner of the sella turcica.
   - It carries the optic nerve.
   - Superior orbital fissure: beside the optic canal, between the lesser and greater wings of the sphenoid.
   - It carries nerves to the eye muscles and sensory nerves to the forehead.

## 5. Mandible

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 7.2 The Skull; 7.5; 9.6; 13.4 · id `mandible` · bone `mandible` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**The lower jaw: the only skull bone that moves (mandible). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The mandible is your lower jaw bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>

*Deeper.* It is one of two unpaired facial bones; the other is the vomer. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1861377]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It forms the lower jaw, below the upper jaw bones (maxillae). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1861377; 7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>
- At the back, each side rises to meet the temporal bone just in front of the ear canal. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1370107; 7.2 ¶fs-id1882965]</sub>
- The hyoid bone lies just below it, in the upper neck. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1399933]</sub>

*Simpler.* It forms your chin and lower jaw, and hinges just in front of your ears. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273; 7.2 ¶fs-id1882965]</sub>

*Deeper.* Each condyle sits in the mandibular fossa of the temporal bone, with a disc between them. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1380884]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Body.** The horizontal, U-shaped part that holds the lower teeth. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>
- **Ramus.** The upright part at the back of each side (“ramus” means branch). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>
- **Angle.** The corner where the body and ramus meet. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>
- **Condylar process.** The back projection, topped by the condyle. It forms the jaw joint with the temporal bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1370107]</sub>
- **Coronoid process.** The flat front projection. A biting muscle attaches to it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1370107]</sub>
- **Mandibular foramen.** An opening on the inner ramus. The nerve and vessels to the lower teeth enter here. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>
- **Mental foramen.** An opening at the front. A sensory nerve to the chin comes out here. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>
- **Alveolar process.** The top edge of the body, which anchors the lower teeth. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>
- **Mylohyoid line.** A ridge inside the body. The muscle forming the floor of the mouth attaches here. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>

*Simpler.* It has a curved body for the teeth and two upright parts that reach up to the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>

*Deeper.* The condylar process forms the jaw joint; the coronoid process anchors a biting muscle. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1370107]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- As the mouth opens, the jaw hinges down and the condyle glides forwards onto a bony bump (articular tubercle). This lets the mouth open wide. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2608267]</sub>
- A flexible disc between the condyle and the temporal bone smooths the movement. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1380884]</sub>
- Its processes and ridges give attachment to the strong chewing muscles. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1370107; 7.2 ¶fs-id1399296]</sub>

*Simpler.* It hinges and slides at the jaw joint, so you can open wide and chew. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2608267]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *joint* **Temporomandibular joint.** The condyle meets the mandibular fossa and articular tubercle of the temporal bone. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1380884]</sub>
- *muscles* **Chewing muscles.** Arise from the zygomatic arch and fill the temporal and infratemporal fossae. They move the mandible. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1285208; 7.2 ¶fs-id1399296]</sub>
- *nerve* **Trigeminal nerve.** Controls the chewing muscles and gives feeling to the face. <sub>[13.4 ¶fs-id1271098]</sub>
- *nerve* **Inferior alveolar nerve.** The nerve to the lower teeth: enters the mandibular foramen. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677; † OpenStax describes the nerve without naming it; “inferior alveolar nerve” needs an approved source.]</sub>
- *ligament* **Ligament to the lingula.** From the base of the skull to the lingula. It steadies the jaw as the mouth opens and closes. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677; 9.6 ¶fs-id2608267]</sub>
- *muscle* **Floor of the mouth.** The muscle forming the floor of the mouth attaches to the mylohyoid lines. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>

*Simpler.* Chewing muscles move it, and nerves to the lower teeth and chin run through it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1399296; 7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>

### 6. Development

- At birth, it is in two halves. They fuse during the first year into one U-shaped bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1891273]</sub>
- In childhood the jaws grow as the teeth come in. This enlarges the face. <sub>[7.5 ¶fs-id1241553]</sub>
- Like other flat bones of the face, most of it forms directly from membrane (intramembranous ossification). † <sub>[6.4 ¶fs-id1804592; † OpenStax says the flat bones of the face develop this way; that this includes most of the mandible needs an approved source.]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- In males the angle of the mandible tends to be squarer and more flared. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** Opening the mouth too wide, as in a large bite, or a blow to the jaw.  
   **Damaged:** The jaw joint dislocates: the condyle moves in front of the articular tubercle.  
   **Patient can’t:** Close the mouth.  
   **Why:** The condyle is stuck in front of the tubercle and cannot slide back. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1933265]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A dental injection near the mandibular foramen.  
   **Damaged:** The sensory nerve to the lower teeth.  
   **Patient can’t:** Feel the lower teeth on that side.  
   **Why:** The nerve is reached before it enters the foramen and runs through the bone to the teeth. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>

3. **Injury:** Arthritis, grinding of the teeth or damage to the disc (temporomandibular joint disorder).  
   **Damaged:** The jaw joint and its disc.  
   **Patient can’t:** Chew comfortably; sometimes move the jaw at all (“lock jaw”).  
   **Why:** The worn or damaged joint surfaces no longer glide smoothly. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1933265]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain how the temporomandibular joint lets the mouth open wide.** <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1380884; 9.6 ¶fs-id2608267]</sub>

   - The mandible hinges downwards.
   - At the same time the condyle and disc glide forwards onto the articular tubercle.
   - The net movement of the condyle is forwards and downwards.
   - The disc smooths the movement between the bones.

2. *Predict.* **A patient yawns widely and cannot close their mouth. Predict where the condyle is.** <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1933265; 9.6 ¶fs-id2608267]</sub>

   - The jaw joint has dislocated.
   - The condyle has moved forwards, in front of the articular tubercle.
   - It cannot slide back into the mandibular fossa.
   - So the mouth stays open.

3. *Compare.* **Compare the mandibular foramen and the mental foramen.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1327677]</sub>

   - Mandibular foramen: on the inner side of the ramus.
   - The nerve and vessels to the lower teeth enter it.
   - Mental foramen: at the front of the body, on each side.
   - A sensory nerve to the chin comes out through it.

*None from an approved source yet; the app says so.*

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** Overuse of the tendons or a wrist injury makes the contents of the carpal tunnel swell.

2. **Injury:** A violent fall onto the hand with the wrist bent far back.

   **Patient can’t:** Move the wrist; the fingers may also be numb.

   - The hamate is hooked and fourth.
   - The capitate meets mainly the third metacarpal; the hamate meets the fourth and fifth.

3. *Predict.* **Predict the effect of a fall that dislocates the capitate backwards around the lunate.** † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

   - The capitate and the hand move backwards behind it.
   - The wrist is deformed and cannot be moved.
   - The median nerve may be pressed on.

## 7. Clavicle

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.1 The Pectoral Girdle; 8.5; 9.4; 20.5 · id `clavicle` · bone `clavicle_left` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**The S-shaped collarbone at the front of the shoulder, joining the arm to the trunk (clavicle). <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1371880]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The clavicle is your collarbone. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1371880]</sub>

*Deeper.* It is the only long bone in the body that lies horizontally. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1192235]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It lies at the front of the shoulder, so you can feel its whole length. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1371880]</sub>
- Its inner (medial) end joins the sternum. Its outer (lateral) end joins the acromion of the scapula. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>
- With the scapula it forms the shoulder (pectoral) girdle. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1371880]</sub>

*Simpler.* It runs across the top of your chest, from your breastbone out to your shoulder. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1371880; 8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>

*Deeper.* Its joint with the sternum is the only bony joint between the upper limb and the axial skeleton. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Sternal end.** The triangular inner end. It meets the manubrium of the sternum at the sternoclavicular joint. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>
- **Acromial end.** The outer end. It meets the acromion at the acromioclavicular joint. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809; 8.1 ¶fs-id1475196]</sub>
- **Shaft.** The curved middle part between the two ends. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>
- **Underside of the acromial end.** The strong coracoclavicular ligament anchors here, tying it to the coracoid process. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>
- **Near the sternal end.** The costoclavicular ligament ties it to the first rib below. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>

*Simpler.* It has an inner end, an outer end and a curved shaft between them. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>

*Deeper.* The rough areas where muscles attach are more marked in manual workers. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- Anchored by muscles from above, it acts as a strut that holds the scapula out to the side. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1192235]</sub>
- This keeps the shoulder joint away from the trunk, giving the arm the widest range of movement. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1192235]</sub>
- It passes forces acting on the upper limb to the sternum and the axial skeleton. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1192235]</sub>
- It shields the nerves and blood vessels passing between the trunk and the upper limb. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1192235]</sub>

*Simpler.* The clavicle props your shoulder out to the side and passes force from your arm to your chest. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1192235]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *joint* **Sternoclavicular joint.** A saddle joint with the manubrium. It lets the clavicle move up, down, forwards and back. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id1837120; 8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>
- *joint* **Acromioclavicular joint.** A plane (gliding) joint with the acromion. Its own ligaments are fairly weak. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id1482998; 8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>
- *ligament* **Coracoclavicular ligament.** A very strong band to the coracoid process. It is the main support of the acromioclavicular joint. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1200622]</sub>
- *ligament* **Costoclavicular ligament.** Ties the sternal end to the first rib and supports the sternoclavicular joint. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>
- *disc* **Articular disc.** A pad of fibrocartilage inside the sternoclavicular joint unites the bones strongly. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id1061087]</sub>
- *artery* **Subclavian artery.** Its name means “under the clavicle”. It becomes the axillary artery as it passes into the armpit. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id2381708; 20.5 ¶fs-id1351620]</sub>
- *vein* **Subclavian vein.** The axillary vein becomes the subclavian vein as it passes into the thorax. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id2931123]</sub>

*Simpler.* It joins the breastbone and the shoulder blade, and important vessels and nerves pass beneath it. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809; 8.1 ¶fs-id1866400]</sub>

### 6. Development

- It forms directly from membrane (intramembranous ossification), with no cartilage model: the only limb or girdle bone to do so. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1375181; 8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>
- The clavicle is the first bone in the body to start ossifying, in the fifth week of development. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1375181]</sub>
- Its ossification is not complete until about the age of 25. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1375181]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- In females, it tends to be shorter, thinner and less curved. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>
- In males, it is heavier, longer and more curved, with rougher areas where muscles attach. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A fall onto the outstretched arms, or a strong blow to the side of the shoulder.  
   **Damaged:** The clavicle breaks, usually between its middle and outer parts. It is the bone broken most often.  
   **Patient can’t:** Hold the arm up: the shoulder drops, and the patient supports the arm with the other hand.  
   **Why:** The strong sternoclavicular joint rarely gives way, so the bone breaks. The weight of the arm then pulls the outer part down. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1866400]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A complete fracture of the clavicle.  
   **Damaged:** The bone only, in most cases. The vessels and nerves beneath it are rarely hurt.  
   **Patient can’t:** Move the shoulder without pain, though feeling and blood supply in the arm are usually normal.  
   **Why:** Muscles across the shoulder pull the fragments forwards and inwards, away from the vessels and nerves. <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1866400]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain why a fall on the outstretched arm breaks the clavicle rather than dislocating the sternoclavicular joint.** <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1866400; 8.1 ¶fs-id1192235]</sub>

   - The sternoclavicular joint is strong and rarely dislocated.
   - Force from the arm passes along the clavicle to the sternum.
   - The bone gives way first, usually between its middle and outer parts.
   - This makes the clavicle the bone broken most often.

2. *Compare.* **Compare how the clavicle and the humerus develop.** <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1375181; 8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>

   - Clavicle: directly from membrane (intramembranous ossification), with no cartilage model.
   - Humerus: a cartilage model replaced by bone (endochondral ossification).
   - The clavicle starts in the fifth week. The humerus has a primary centre in its shaft by the twelfth week.
   - The clavicle is not fully ossified until about 25.

3. *Predict.* **Predict how a patient holds their arm after a complete fracture of the clavicle, and explain why.** <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id1866400]</sub>

   - The shoulder and outer fragment drop under the weight of the arm.
   - The patient supports the sagging arm with the other hand.
   - Muscles pull the outer fragment forwards and inwards.
   - So the two fragments override each other.

   *The sternoclavicular joint, between the sternal end of the clavicle and the manubrium, is the only one.* <sub>[8.1 ¶fs-id2241809]</sub>

## 8. Ethmoid bone

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 7.2 The Skull; 7.5; 14.1 · id `ethmoid` · bone `ethmoid_bone` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**A single midline bone between the orbits that forms the roof and upper walls of the nasal cavity (ethmoid bone). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1971405]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The ethmoid bone sits behind the top of your nose, between your eyes. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1971405; 7.2 ¶fs-id1897403]</sub>

*Deeper.* The nerve fibres for smell pass through its cribriform plate into the brain. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977415; 14.1 ¶fs-id2351449]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It lies in the midline, between the orbit and the upper nasal cavity on each side. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1971405; 7.2 ¶fs-id1897403]</sub>
- Inside the skull, it forms a small midline area of the floor of the anterior cranial fossa. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977415]</sub>
- It forms the upper nasal septum and part of the medial wall of each orbit. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1971405]</sub>

*Simpler.* It is deep behind the bridge of your nose, between your eye sockets. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1971405; 7.2 ¶fs-id1897403]</sub>

*Deeper.* Though counted with the brain case, it also helps form the nasal septum, nasal walls and orbits. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1861377]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Crista galli.** A small upward projection in the midline (“rooster’s comb”). It anchors one of the coverings of the brain. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977415]</sub>
- **Cribriform plates.** Flat plates either side of it (“sieve”), pierced by small olfactory foramina. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977415]</sub>
- **Perpendicular plate.** Forms the upper part of the nasal septum. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2228601; 7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>
- **Superior and middle nasal conchae.** Thin curved plates projecting from each side wall of the nasal cavity. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2228601; 7.2 ¶fs-id1475747]</sub>
- **Ethmoid air cells.** Many small air spaces with very thin walls, between the nasal cavity and the orbit. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1897403; 7.2 ¶fs-id1882870]</sub>
- **Orbital surface.** Forms much of the medial wall of each orbit, with the lacrimal bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1897403; 7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

*Simpler.* It has a sieve-like roof, a central plate in the nose and two scroll-like plates on each side. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977415; 7.2 ¶fs-id2228601; 7.2 ¶fs-id1475747]</sub>

*Deeper.* The superior concha is the smallest; it is hidden above and behind the middle concha. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1475747]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- Its cribriform plates are pierced by olfactory foramina, letting the fibres for smell reach the brain. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977415; 14.1 ¶fs-id2351449]</sub>
- Its curved conchae swirl incoming air, helping to warm and moisten it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1475747]</sub>
- Mucus on the conchae traps dust, pollen, bacteria and viruses. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1475747]</sub>
- Its air cells are part of the paranasal sinuses, which lighten the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1897403; 7.2 ¶fs-id1977054]</sub>

*Simpler.* It lets the nerves for smell through, and helps warm and clean the air you breathe. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977415; 7.2 ¶fs-id1475747]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *nerve* **Olfactory nerve.** Its fibres pass from the nasal lining through the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb. <sub>[14.1 ¶fs-id2351449; 7.2 ¶fs-id1977415]</sub>
- *brain* **Frontal lobe.** The olfactory bulb lies on its underside, just above the cribriform plate. <sub>[14.1 ¶fs-id2351449]</sub>
- *bone* **Vomer.** With the perpendicular plate and the septal cartilage, forms the nasal septum. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>
- *bone* **Lacrimal bone.** Together they form much of the medial wall of the orbit. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>
- *bone* **Frontal bone.** Forms most of the floor of the anterior cranial fossa, around the ethmoid’s small midline part. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2045358]</sub>
- *covering* **Covering of the brain.** One of the layers covering the brain is anchored to the crista galli. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977415]</sub>

*Simpler.* The nerves for smell pass through it to the brain, and it joins the bones of the nose and orbit. <sub>[14.1 ¶fs-id2351449; 7.2 ¶fs-id2125356; 7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

### 6. Development

- Like the bones of the skull base, it forms from a cartilage model (endochondral ossification). <sub>[7.5 ¶fs-id2251103]</sub>
- The paranasal sinuses, including its air cells, enlarge during childhood. <sub>[7.5 ¶fs-id1241553]</sub>

### 7. Variations

*None from an approved source yet; the app says so.*

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** Blunt force to the face, as in a car accident.  
   **Damaged:** The olfactory nerve fibres are sheared where they pass through the cribriform plate.  
   **Patient can’t:** Smell (anosmia); food may taste bland.  
   **Why:** The frontal lobe moves relative to the ethmoid bone, tearing the fine fibres. <sub>[14.1 ¶fs-id2817840; 14.1 ¶fs-id2441552]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A fracture through the cribriform plate.  
   **Damaged:** The coverings of the brain over the plate tear.  
   **Patient can’t:** Keep the nose dry: the clear fluid around the brain (cerebrospinal fluid) leaks from the nose.  
   **Why:** The thin plate is all that separates the nasal cavity from the cranial cavity. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977415; † Leakage of cerebrospinal fluid from the nose is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20); the plate’s position is.]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain how a blow to the face can cause loss of smell.** <sub>[14.1 ¶fs-id2351449; 14.1 ¶fs-id2817840]</sub>

   - Olfactory nerve fibres pass through the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone.
   - They reach the olfactory bulb on the underside of the frontal lobe.
   - In a blow, the frontal lobe moves relative to the ethmoid bone.
   - The fibres are sheared, causing anosmia.

2. *Compare.* **Compare the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid with the vomer.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>

   - Both form part of the bony nasal septum.
   - The perpendicular plate forms its upper part.
   - The triangular vomer forms its lower and back parts.
   - Septal cartilage fills the gap between them at the front.

3. *Predict.* **Predict which nasal conchae you would see looking into the front of a dry skull, and explain why.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1475747]</sub>

   - The inferior and middle conchae.
   - The inferior concha is the largest.
   - The middle concha, part of the ethmoid, lies just above it.
   - The small superior concha is hidden above and behind the middle one.

## 9. Frontal bone

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 7.2 The Skull; 7.5; 9.2; 14.1 · id `frontal` · bone `frontal_bone` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**The single bone of the forehead, which also forms the roof of each orbit (frontal bone). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The frontal bone is your forehead bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>

*Deeper.* Inside the skull, it forms most of the floor of the anterior cranial fossa. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984; 7.2 ¶fs-id2045358]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It forms the forehead, at the front of the brain case. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984; 7.2 ¶fs-id1351960]</sub>
- Behind, it meets the two parietal bones at the coronal suture. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- Below, it forms the upper margin and the roof of each orbit. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984; 7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

*Simpler.* It forms your forehead and the top of your eye sockets. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>

*Deeper.* It is one of the eight brain case bones, and one of four that meet at the pterion. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1351960; 7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Glabella.** A slight hollow between the eyebrows, at the front midline. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>
- **Supraorbital margin.** The upper rim of each orbit. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>
- **Supraorbital foramen.** An opening near the middle of each margin, for a sensory nerve to the forehead. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>
- **Brow ridges.** Rounded thickenings just above each margin, behind the eyebrows. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>
- **Orbital part.** A flat region that forms the roof of the orbits and the floor of the anterior cranial fossa. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>
- **Frontal sinus.** An air-filled space just above the eyebrows: the most anterior paranasal sinus. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1882870]</sub>

*Simpler.* It has a curved front, rims over the eyes and a flat part extending back inside the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>

*Deeper.* The frontal sinus may be one space, or divided at the midline into left and right. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1882870]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- Its curved front encloses the frontal lobes of the brain, which lie in the anterior cranial fossa. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2045358; 7.2 ¶fs-id1256378]</sub>
- The thickened rims of the orbits help protect the eyes. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1883481; 7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>
- Its sinus lightens the skull and adds resonance to the voice. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977054; 7.2 ¶fs-id1882870]</sub>

*Simpler.* It shields the front of the brain and the tops of the eyes. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2045358; 7.2 ¶fs-id1883481]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *suture* **Coronal suture.** Joins it to the right and left parietal bones, across the top of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- *landmark* **Pterion.** Where the frontal, parietal, temporal and sphenoid bones meet: the weakest part of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- *brain* **Frontal lobes.** Lie in the anterior cranial fossa, which the frontal bone mostly floors. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2045358]</sub>
- *bone* **Ethmoid bone.** Its crista galli and cribriform plates lie in the midline of the same floor. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2045358]</sub>
- *nerve* **Sensory nerve to the forehead.** Passes through the supraorbital foramen. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>
- *space* **Orbit.** The frontal bone forms its roof. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

*Simpler.* It joins the parietal bones behind and roofs the eye sockets below. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497; 7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

### 6. Development

- Like the other bones of the top and sides of the brain case, it forms directly from membrane (intramembranous ossification). <sub>[7.5 ¶fs-id1547037]</sub>
- At birth, it is in two halves, which fuse in early childhood. <sub>[7.5 ¶fs-id1241553]</sub>
- The anterior fontanelle, the soft spot at the front of a baby’s head, lies where it meets the parietal bones. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- The brow ridges vary in size and are generally larger in males. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984]</sub>
- The frontal sinus may be a single space or two. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1882870]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A cold or sinus congestion.  
   **Damaged:** The lining of the frontal sinus swells and blocks its narrow passage to the nasal cavity.  
   **Patient can’t:** Be free of pressure and pain above the eyebrows.  
   **Why:** The blocked sinus fills with fluid, and the pressure causes pain. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1977054; 7.2 ¶fs-id1882870]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A strong blow to the forehead.  
   **Damaged:** The bone breaks; in a depressed fracture, it is pushed inwards.  
   **Patient can’t:** Escape the risk of bleeding inside the skull, which can injure the brain.  
   **Why:** The frontal lobes lie directly behind the bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1972896; 7.2 ¶fs-id2045358]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain how the frontal bone takes part in both the orbit and the cranial cavity.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984; 7.2 ¶fs-id1887927; 7.2 ¶fs-id2045358]</sub>

   - Its front forms the forehead and the supraorbital margins.
   - Inside the skull it extends backwards as a flat region.
   - This region forms the roof of the orbits below.
   - And it forms most of the floor of the anterior cranial fossa above.

2. *Compare.* **Compare the frontal bone with the parietal bones.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1283984; 7.2 ¶fs-id1975652; 7.2 ¶fs-id1248497; 7.5 ¶fs-id1547037]</sub>

   - The frontal bone is single and forms the forehead.
   - The parietal bones are paired and form most of the upper sides of the skull.
   - They meet at the coronal suture.
   - All of them form directly from membrane.

3. *Predict.* **Predict where a patient with a blocked frontal sinus feels pain, and explain why.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1882870; 7.2 ¶fs-id1977054]</sub>

   - Just above the eyebrows, where the frontal sinus lies.
   - Swollen lining blocks the narrow passage to the nasal cavity.
   - The sinus fills with fluid.
   - The rising pressure causes pain.

   A. Sagittal · **B. Coronal** · C. Lambdoid · D. Squamous  
   *The coronal suture runs across the skull between them.* <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

## 11. Phalanges of the hand

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb (and glossary); 8.5; 9.4; 9.5; 20.5 · id `hand-phalanges` · bone `hand_proximal_phalanx_3_left` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**The 14 bones of the fingers and thumb (phalanges). <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490; glossary: phalanx bone of the hand]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The phalanges are the bones of your fingers and thumb. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>

*Deeper.* The name comes from the Greek “phalanx”, a rectangular block of soldiers. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- The thumb (digit 1) has two: a proximal and a distal phalanx. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>
- Each finger, digits 2 to 5, has three: proximal, middle and distal. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>
- The proximal phalanges meet the heads of the metacarpals at the knuckles. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1242304]</sub>

*Simpler.* There are two in your thumb and three in each finger. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>

*Deeper.* The anatomical name for the thumb is the pollex. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490; glossary: pollex]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Proximal phalanges.** The nearest to the palm; each meets a metacarpal head at a knuckle joint. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490; 8.2 ¶fs-id1242304]</sub>
- **Middle phalanges.** Only in the fingers, between the proximal and distal phalanges. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>
- **Distal phalanges.** The tips of the fingers and thumb. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>
- **Interphalangeal joints.** The joints between neighbouring phalanges. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490; glossary: interphalangeal joint]</sub>
- **Base, shaft and head.** Each phalanx has a base nearest the palm, a shaft and a head. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* Each finger has a near, a middle and an end bone; the thumb has no middle one. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>

*Deeper.* So the thumb has one interphalangeal joint, and each finger has two. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- Interphalangeal joints are hinge joints: they only bend and straighten. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id2523908]</sub>
- Knuckle (metacarpophalangeal) joints are condyloid, so the fingers can bend and also spread apart. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id1434553]</sub>
- Spreading the fingers apart is abduction; bringing them together is adduction. <sub>[9.5 ¶fs-id1751608]</sub>

*Simpler.* Knuckle joints bend and spread; the joints along the fingers only bend. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id1434553; 9.4 ¶fs-id2523908]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *joints* **Metacarpophalangeal (knuckle) joints.** Condyloid joints between the metacarpal heads and the proximal phalanges. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id1434553; glossary: metacarpophalangeal joint]</sub>
- *joints* **Interphalangeal joints.** Hinge joints between neighbouring phalanges. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id2523908; glossary: interphalangeal joint]</sub>
- *bones* **Metacarpals.** Meet the proximal phalanges at the knuckles. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1242304]</sub>
- *arteries* **Digital arteries.** Branch from the palmar arches to supply the fingers. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id1595346]</sub>
- *veins* **Digital veins.** Drain the fingers into the palmar venous arches. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id3239091]</sub>

*Simpler.* They hinge on each other and join the palm bones at the knuckles. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id2523908; 8.2 ¶fs-id1242304]</sub>

### 6. Development

- They form by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification). <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>
- Small bones like these develop only one secondary ossification centre, so each has one growth plate. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1972676]</sub>
- A growth plate is cartilage, less dense than bone, so it looks dark on an X-ray. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1972676]</sub>

### 7. Variations

*None from an approved source yet; the app says so.*

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A child’s injured finger has an X-ray.  
   **Damaged:** There may be no injury: each phalanx has a normal growth plate.  
   **Patient can’t:** Rely on one dark line alone to diagnose a fracture.  
   **Why:** Cartilage is less dense than bone, so a normal plate looks dark and can be mistaken for a break. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1972676]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A ball strikes the tip of a straight finger.  
   **Damaged:** The tendon that straightens the fingertip tears from the distal phalanx, sometimes with a fragment of bone (mallet finger).  
   **Patient can’t:** Straighten the tip of the finger.  
   **Why:** Without its tendon, nothing pulls the distal phalanx straight. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain why the thumb has one interphalangeal joint, but each finger has two.** <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>

   - The thumb has two phalanges: proximal and distal.
   - Each finger has three: proximal, middle and distal.
   - An interphalangeal joint lies between each pair of neighbouring phalanges.
   - So the thumb has one, and each finger has two.

2. *Compare.* **Compare the knuckle (metacarpophalangeal) joints with the interphalangeal joints.** <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id1434553; 9.4 ¶fs-id2523908]</sub>

   - Knuckle joints are condyloid: biaxial.
   - They allow bending and straightening, and spreading and closing the fingers.
   - Interphalangeal joints are hinge joints: uniaxial.
   - They allow only bending and straightening.

3. *Predict.* **A cricket ball strikes the tip of a straight finger, and the tip now droops. Predict the injury and explain it.** † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

   - Mallet finger.
   - The tendon that straightens the fingertip has torn from the distal phalanx.
   - A small piece of bone may come away with it.
   - Nothing now pulls the fingertip straight.

## 17. Metacarpal bones

- **Shaft.** The long middle part of each bone. <sub>[glossary: metacarpal bone]</sub>

- The first metacarpal is separate, so the thumb can move freely on its own. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1436751]</sub>
- The second and third are fixed, giving the palm a firm middle. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1436751]</sub>
- The fourth and fifth move forwards in a power grip, pressing the inner hand against the object. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1436751]</sub>

- *joints* **Carpometacarpal joints.** Between the second row of carpal bones and the bases of the metacarpals. <sub>[glossary: carpometacarpal joint]</sub>
- *joint* **First carpometacarpal joint.** A saddle joint between the trapezium and the first metacarpal. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id1837120]</sub>
- *joints* **Metacarpophalangeal (knuckle) joints.** Condyloid joints between the metacarpal heads and the proximal phalanges. <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id1434553]</sub>
- *bones* **Carpal bones.** The trapezium carries the first and second, and the trapezoid the second. The capitate carries mainly the third, and the hamate the fourth and fifth. <sub>[glossary: trapezium; glossary: trapezoid; glossary: capitate; glossary: hamate]</sub>
- *arteries* **Palmar arches.** The radial and ulnar arteries join at the wrist to form the palmar arches that supply the hand. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id1595346]</sub>
- *veins* **Palmar venous arches.** The digital veins join in the hand to form the palmar venous arches. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id3239091]</sub>

*Simpler.* They join the wrist bones above and the finger bones below. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1242304]</sub>

### 6. Development

- They form by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification). <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>
- Each has two ossification centres: one for the shaft, and one for the head (in the first, for the base). † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20). Gray’s 1918 (8.2) gives two centres for each metacarpal (supporting only).]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- The first metacarpal ossifies like a phalanx, so some anatomists count the thumb as having three phalanges. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20). Gray’s 1918 (8.2) gives two centres for each metacarpal (supporting only).]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A punch that lands on a hard object.

   **Damaged:** The base of the first metacarpal breaks into the saddle joint (Bennett fracture).  
   **Patient can’t:** Pinch or touch the thumb to the fingertips without pain.  
   **Why:** The break enters the saddle joint on which opposition depends. † <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id1837120; 9.5 ¶fs-id1354267; † The fracture is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20); the saddle joint and opposition are.]</sub>

   - So it moves within the plane of the palm and out in front of it.
   - Flexion with abduction here produces opposition.

3. *Predict.* **A man punches a wall and the knuckle of his little finger sinks. Predict the injury and explain it.** † <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1436751; † Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

   - A fracture of the neck of the fifth metacarpal (boxer’s fracture).
   - The head tips towards the palm.
   - So the knuckle sinks when he makes a fist.
   - The fifth metacarpal is the most mobile and least protected.

   - The horizontal plates of the palatine bones join behind them.
   - All four fuse in the midline to form the hard palate.

   - It affects about 1 in 1000 births, mostly males.
   - Cleft palate: the two halves of the hard palate fail to fuse; about 1 in 2500 births, more often females.
   - Cleft palate is more severe, as it makes suckling difficult.

## 22. Parietal bone

### 1. In one line

**One of a pair of curved bones forming most of the upper sides and top of the skull (parietal bone). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1975652]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The parietal bones form the top and sides of your head. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1975652]</sub>

*Deeper.* The two parietal bones meet along the top of the skull at the sagittal suture. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It forms most of the upper side of the skull; the right and left meet at the top. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1975652]</sub>
- The frontal bone is in front, the temporal bone below and the occipital bone behind. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1975652]</sub>
- It is one of the flat bones that form the top and sides of the brain case. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1256378]</sub>

*Simpler.* It forms most of the top and side of your head. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1975652]</sub>

*Deeper.* The parietal is one of the four bones that meet at the pterion, the weakest part of the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Sagittal border.** Meets the other parietal bone at the sagittal suture. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- **Frontal border.** Meets the frontal bone at the coronal suture. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- **Occipital border.** Meets the occipital bone at the lambdoid suture. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- **Squamous border.** Meets the squamous part of the temporal bone at the squamous suture. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- **Grooves on the inner surface.** Branches of the artery that supplies the brain’s coverings mark the inner surface of the skull. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2026215; † OpenStax says the artery’s branches groove the inner surface of the skull; that this includes the parietal bone needs an approved source.]</sub>

*Simpler.* It is a curved plate of bone, joined to its neighbours by sutures. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1975652; 7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

*Deeper.* Its sutures twist tightly, interlocking it with its neighbours to strengthen the skull. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1195048]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- As a curved flat bone of the brain case, it encloses and protects the brain. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1256378]</sub>
- Its twisting sutures interlock with the neighbouring bones, adding strength. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1195048]</sub>
- Sutures are fibrous joints that allow almost no movement. <sub>[9.2 ¶fs-id2663393]</sub>

*Simpler.* It is a strong, curved shield over the brain. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1256378]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *suture* **Sagittal suture.** Unites the right and left parietal bones along the midline. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- *suture* **Coronal suture.** Joins it to the frontal bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- *suture* **Lambdoid suture.** Joins it to the occipital bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- *suture* **Squamous suture.** Joins it to the temporal bone. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>
- *vein* **Superior sagittal sinus.** A large vein of the brain’s coverings, running in the midline beneath the top of the skull. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id977085]</sub>

*Simpler.* Sutures join it to the frontal, occipital and temporal bones, and to its partner. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

### 6. Development

- It forms directly from membrane (intramembranous ossification), like the other bones of the top and sides of the brain case. <sub>[7.5 ¶fs-id1547037]</sub>
- In a baby, wide areas of connective tissue (fontanelles) separate it from its neighbours. <sub>[9.2 ¶fs-id2293668; 7.5 ¶fs-id1547037]</sub>
- The fontanelles narrow greatly during the first year, as the bones enlarge, and become sutures. <sub>[9.2 ¶fs-id2293668]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** The sagittal suture closes too early (craniosynostosis).  
   **Damaged:** The joint between the two parietal bones fuses.  
   **Patient can’t:** Grow across the top of the head: the head becomes long and narrow (scaphocephaly).  
   **Why:** Growth is diverted to the sides of the skull, which grow upwards. <sub>[7.5 ¶fs-id1031698; 7.5 ¶fs-id2339105]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A blow to the side of the head.  
   **Damaged:** A linear fracture, with lines radiating from the point of impact.  
   **Patient can’t:** Escape the risk of bleeding inside the skull, which can injure the brain.  
   **Why:** A fracture of the brain case can tear the vessels inside it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1972896]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain why a newborn’s skull can change shape during birth.** <sub>[9.2 ¶fs-id2293668; 7.5 ¶fs-id1547037]</sub>

   - The brain case bones are still separated by wide fontanelles.
   - These are areas of dense connective tissue.
   - They let the bones push closer together or overlap slightly.
   - So the head can pass through the birth canal.

2. *Compare.* **Compare a suture with a fontanelle.** <sub>[9.2 ¶fs-id2663393; 9.2 ¶fs-id2293668]</sub>

   - Both are fibrous joints between skull bones.
   - A fontanelle is a wide area of connective tissue in a baby’s skull.
   - A suture is a narrow, convoluted fibrous joint in the adult skull.
   - Fontanelles narrow into sutures as the bones grow.

3. *Predict.* **Predict the head shape if the sagittal suture fuses too early, and explain it.** <sub>[7.5 ¶fs-id1031698]</sub>

   - The skull cannot widen across the top.
   - Growth is diverted to the sides, which grow upwards.
   - The head becomes long, narrow and wedge-shaped (scaphocephaly).
   - The brain still has room to grow normally.

- *ligament* **Annular ligament.** Encircles the head and holds it against the ulna as it turns. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2602265]</sub>
- *membrane* **Interosseous membrane.** A sheet of dense connective tissue joining the shafts of the radius and ulna. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1266017; 8.2 ¶fs-id1411842]</sub>
- *artery* **Radial artery.** Runs down the forearm alongside the radius to the wrist. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id1595346]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** An injury to the joint at the base of the thumb.  
   **Damaged:** The first carpometacarpal (saddle) joint.  
   **Patient can’t:** Touch the tip of the thumb to the tip of a finger (opposition).

   **Patient can’t:** Pinch, open jars or turn keys without pain at the base of the thumb.

*None from an approved source yet; the app says so.*

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** Overuse of the tendons or a wrist injury makes the contents of the carpal tunnel swell.

   **Patient can’t:** Bend the wrist back fully without pain over the lump.  
   **Why:** The lump catches the tendons that run over the back of the wrist. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

## 29. Ulna

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb; 8.5; 9.4; 9.5; 9.6; 20.5 · id `ulna` · bone `ulna_left` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**The medial bone of the forearm, on the little-finger side (ulna). <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 8.2 ¶fs-id1415448]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The ulna is the forearm bone on the little-finger side. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 8.2 ¶fs-id2002490]</sub>

*Deeper.* Its hooked upper end grips the humerus and forms the hinge of the elbow. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 9.6 ¶fs-id1355969]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It runs from the elbow to the wrist, parallel to the radius, on the medial side. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518]</sub>
- Its upper end forms the bony point of the elbow. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518]</sub>
- At the wrist it does not touch the carpal bones directly. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>

*Simpler.* It runs from the point of your elbow down to the little-finger side of your wrist. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518]</sub>

*Deeper.* A pad of fibrocartilage between the radius and the ulna’s styloid process meets the triquetrum. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Trochlear notch.** A large C-shaped hollow, like a crescent spanner. It wraps around the trochlea of the humerus. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518]</sub>
- **Olecranon.** The bony point of the elbow. It fits into the olecranon fossa of the humerus when the elbow straightens. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>
- **Coronoid process.** The lip below the notch. It fits into the coronoid fossa when the elbow bends fully. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>
- **Radial notch.** A small smooth area beside the notch, where the head of the radius turns. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518]</sub>
- **Ulnar tuberosity.** A rough area on the front, just below the coronoid process. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518]</sub>
- **Interosseous border.** A ridge on the outer side of the shaft. The interosseous membrane attaches along it. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1266017]</sub>
- **Head and styloid process.** The small rounded lower end, with a short projection behind it. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1266017]</sub>

*Simpler.* Its big hooked top end wraps around the humerus; its small head is at the wrist. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 8.2 ¶fs-id1266017]</sub>

*Deeper.* The ulna’s head is at its lower end. The radius is the other way round: its head is at the elbow. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1266017; 8.2 ¶fs-id1411842]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- The C-shaped notch wraps around the pulley-shaped trochlea, so the elbow can only bend and straighten. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 9.4 ¶fs-id2523908]</sub>
- The olecranon and coronoid process fit into hollows of the humerus. So the elbow can straighten and bend fully. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2444175]</sub>
- In pronation, it is the radius that turns; its lower end crosses over the ulna. <sub>[9.5 ¶fs-id1837207]</sub>

*Simpler.* Its hook makes a strong hinge, so your elbow bends and straightens like a door. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 9.4 ¶fs-id2523908]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *joint* **Elbow (humeroulnar) joint.** A uniaxial hinge joint between the trochlea and the trochlear notch. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1355969; 9.4 ¶fs-id2523908]</sub>
- *joint* **Proximal radioulnar joint.** The radial notch meets the head of the radius. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518]</sub>
- *joint* **Distal radioulnar joint.** Its head meets the ulnar notch of the radius. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1411842]</sub>
- *ligament* **Ulnar collateral ligament.** A triangular band from the medial epicondyle to the inner side of the upper ulna. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2430675]</sub>
- *membrane* **Interosseous membrane.** Joins the shafts of the ulna and radius. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1266017]</sub>
- *artery* **Ulnar artery.** Runs down the forearm alongside the ulna to the wrist. <sub>[20.5 ¶fs-id1595346]</sub>
- *nerve* **Ulnar nerve.** Passes behind the medial epicondyle and enters the forearm beside the ulna. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* It hinges with the humerus and joins the radius at both ends. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id1355969; 8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 8.2 ¶fs-id1411842]</sub>

### 6. Development

- It forms by replacing a cartilage model with bone (endochondral ossification). <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>
- Bone first appears in the shaft by about the twelfth week. Centres in the ends appear later, usually after birth. <sub>[8.5 ¶fs-id1397163]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- With the elbow straight, the forearm angles 5–15 degrees outwards from the arm (the carrying angle). <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1483561]</sub>
- The carrying angle is larger in females, to clear their wider pelvis. <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id1483561]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** Repeated forceful straightening of the forearm, as in baseball pitching.  
   **Damaged:** The ulnar collateral ligament on the inner side of the elbow.  
   **Patient can’t:** Throw forcefully; the ligament may need reconstruction (“Tommy John surgery”).  
   **Why:** This ligament stops the elbow moving sideways and over-straightening; its front part resists over-straightening most. <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2430675]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A fall onto the point of the elbow.  
   **Damaged:** The olecranon breaks.  
   **Patient can’t:** Straighten the elbow against resistance.  
   **Why:** The muscle that straightens the elbow (triceps) pulls on the olecranon, drawing the fragment away. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 9. Tutorial questions

1. *Explain.* **Explain why the elbow can only bend and straighten.** <sub>[9.4 ¶fs-id2523908; 8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 9.6 ¶fs-id2430675]</sub>

   - The humeroulnar joint is a hinge joint.
   - The C-shaped trochlear notch wraps around the pulley-shaped trochlea.
   - This allows movement around a single axis.
   - Strong collateral ligaments also prevent side-to-side movement.

2. *Compare.* **Compare the upper and lower ends of the ulna and the radius.** <sub>[8.2 ¶fs-id2080518; 8.2 ¶fs-id1266017; 8.2 ¶fs-id1411842; 8.2 ¶fs-id1882937]</sub>

   - Ulna: a large upper end with the trochlear notch, and a small head below.
   - Radius: a small disc-shaped head above, and a broad lower end.
   - The lower end of the radius meets two carpal bones.
   - The lower end of the ulna does not touch the carpal bones directly.

3. *Predict.* **A baseball pitcher has pain on the inner side of the elbow when throwing. Predict the injured structure and its normal job.** <sub>[9.6 ¶fs-id2430675]</sub>

   - The ulnar collateral ligament.
   - It runs from the medial epicondyle to the inner side of the upper ulna.
   - It stops sideways movement and over-straightening of the elbow.
   - Forceful repeated throwing strains it; it may need reconstruction.

## 30. Vomer

*OpenStax A&P 2e: 7.2 The Skull; 6.4 · id `vomer` · bone `vomer` · draft*

### 1. In one line

**A single, triangular bone that forms the lower and back part of the nasal septum (vomer). <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1254996]</sub>**

*Simpler.* The vomer is a bone in the middle of your nose, at the lower back. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1254996]</sub>

*Deeper.* Seen from behind, it forms the whole height of the nasal septum. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1254996]</sub>

### 2. Where it is

- It lies in the midline, in the lower and back part of the nasal septum. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1254996]</sub>
- Above it is the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid; in front is the septal cartilage. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>
- It is one of only two unpaired facial bones; the other is the mandible. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1861377]</sub>

*Simpler.* It is in the middle of your nose, at the back and bottom. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1254996]</sub>

*Deeper.* Looking into the front of the nose, you see only a small part of it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1254996]</sub>

### 3. Key features

- **Triangular shape.** A flat, triangular plate in the midline. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1254996]</sub>
- **Upper edge.** Lies below the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>
- **Front edge.** The septal cartilage fills the gap between it and the perpendicular plate. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>
- **Back edge.** Divides the two back openings of the nasal cavity. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1254996]</sub>

*Simpler.* It is a flat triangle of bone standing upright in the middle of the nose. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1254996]</sub>

*Deeper.* In the dry skull, the septal cartilage is missing, leaving a gap in front of it. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>

### 4. Form → function

- With the ethmoid and the septal cartilage, it divides the nasal cavity into right and left halves. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1211287; 7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>
- The septal cartilage at its front edge extends into the nose and separates the nostrils. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>

*Simpler.* It helps split your nose into two separate airways. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1211287; 7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *bone* **Ethmoid bone.** Its perpendicular plate forms the upper part of the septum. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>
- *cartilage* **Septal cartilage.** Forms the front of the septum, between the vomer and the perpendicular plate. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>
- *space* **Nasal cavity.** The septum divides it into two halves. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1211287]</sub>
- *bone* **Sphenoid bone.** Its upper border meets the body of the sphenoid. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

*Simpler.* It forms the nasal septum with part of the ethmoid and a plate of cartilage. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>

### 6. Development

- Like the other flat bones of the face, it forms directly from membrane (intramembranous ossification). † <sub>[6.4 ¶fs-id1804592; † OpenStax 6.4 says the flat bones of the face form from membrane, but 7.5 says the facial bones form from cartilage; which applies to the vomer needs an approved source.]</sub>
- It forms from two thin plates, which are almost completely united by puberty. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20). Gray’s 1918 (7.2) says so (supporting only).]</sub>

### 7. Variations

- The nasal septum is often bent to one side (a deviated septum). † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

### 8. Clinical reasoning

1. **Injury:** A septum bent to one side, from development or an injury.  
   **Damaged:** The bony and cartilage parts of the septum, including the vomer.  
   **Patient can’t:** Breathe freely through the narrower side.  
   **Why:** The septum divides the nasal cavity, so bending it narrows one side. † <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1211287; † A deviated septum is not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20); the septum’s role is.]</sub>

2. **Injury:** A blow to the nose that breaks the septum.  
   **Damaged:** The septal cartilage and the vomer.  
   **Patient can’t:** Breathe evenly through both sides of the nose.  
   **Why:** The broken septum shifts into one side of the nasal cavity. † <sub>[† Not in OpenStax A&P 2e (chapters 6–9, 12–16, 20).]</sub>

   - The cartilage also separates the nostrils, and is absent in the dry skull.

3. *Predict.* **Predict what the nasal septum of a dry skull looks like from the front, and explain why.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2125356]</sub>

## 31. Zygomatic bone

*Deeper.* The pterion lies a thumb’s width behind its upward part, about two finger widths above the arch. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

### 3. Key features

*Deeper.* The arch works like a drawbridge: two processes, one from each bone, meet in the middle. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1285208]</sub>

- The lateral wall of each orbit angles 45 degrees away from the midline, widening side (peripheral) vision. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1883481]</sub>
- The thickened margins of the orbit help protect the eye. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1883481]</sub>
- The arch it helps form is a bridge of bone from which a major chewing muscle arises. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1285208]</sub>

*Simpler.* It shapes your cheek, guards the side of your eye and anchors a chewing muscle. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id2159036; 7.2 ¶fs-id1883481; 7.2 ¶fs-id1285208]</sub>

### 5. Relations

- *bone* **Temporal bone.** Its zygomatic process joins the temporal process to complete the arch. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1285208]</sub>
- *space* **Orbit.** Forms its lateral wall and lateral floor; the maxilla forms the inner floor. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>
- *muscle* **Chewing muscle.** A major muscle that pulls the mandible up in biting and chewing arises from the arch. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1285208]</sub>
- *spaces* **Temporal and infratemporal fossae.** The spaces above and below the arch, filled by chewing muscles. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1399296]</sub>

*Simpler.* It joins the temporal bone in the arch and forms the outer wall of the eye socket. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1285208; 7.2 ¶fs-id1887927]</sub>

### 7. Variations

*None from an approved source yet; the app says so.*

### 8. Clinical reasoning

   **Patient can’t:** Be left without close watching: a haematoma can build up over hours.  
   **Why:** The pterion is the weakest part of the skull, and bleeding there presses on the brain. <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497; 7.2 ¶fs-id1364278]</sub>

   - The lateral wall angles outwards, widening side vision.

3. *Predict.* **Predict how to find the pterion on a living head, using the zygomatic bone.** <sub>[7.2 ¶fs-id1248497]</sub>

   - This is the pterion, the weakest part of the skull.
